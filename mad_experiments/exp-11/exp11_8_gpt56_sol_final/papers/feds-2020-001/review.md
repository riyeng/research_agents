## EDITORIAL RATIONALE
Decision: MAJOR REVISION REQUIRED. The strongest credible version of the paper is a historical measurement and forecast-encompassing study showing that sentiment in internal Federal Reserve forecast narratives contains information beyond accompanying point forecasts. The transparent construction of Tonality, the Greenbook setting, comparison with Federal Reserve and Blue Chip forecasts, and distributional analysis constitute a coherent contribution. Novelty and insight are both medium: the institutional application and empirical package are distinctive, but the text methods and predictive designs are established, and the manuscript itself identifies close antecedents in sentiment, central-bank communication, and forecast-narrative research.

The principal substantive flaw concerns the prospective interpretation. The manuscript describes pseudo-out-of-sample gains as evidence of a material real-time benefit, yet it does not demonstrate that the complete feature pipeline was fixed using only information available at each forecast origin. Trend Tonality's decay parameter is justified partly by fit over the observed series; the domain-adjusted dictionary has no documented development/evaluation split; normalization is described over the full sample; and early corpus construction uses future documents. Full-sample affine standardization is largely innocuous in regressions with intercepts, and the early-corpus issue may not directly contaminate an evaluation beginning after 64 observations. The decisive concern is narrower: the smoothing choice and overall feature-development protocol were not demonstrably frozen before evaluation. This does not invalidate the historical in-sample associations, but it prevents the reported exercise from establishing real-time forecasting or investment value.

A second substantive issue is inference under overlapping horizons and changing forecast frequency. The manuscript refers both to a fixed rule of 2k+1 lags and automatic Newey-West bandwidth selection, although Greenbooks were nearly monthly before 1981 and eight per year thereafter. Observation-lag rules tied to quarterly horizons may not cover the same calendar-time dependence across regimes. The revision should explain the procedure precisely and establish robustness using calendar-time-aware bandwidths, longer dependence allowances, or frequency-specific estimates.

There is no unresolved blocker, and both defects appear repairable within the existing framework. The central rephrasal issues are also important but distinct from these execution problems. Predictive stock-return regressions establish return predictability; they do not identify delayed incorporation of private cash-flow information rather than time-varying expected returns or omitted state variables. The downside-risk conclusion should be outcome-specific: the record supports especially strong asymmetry for unemployment, while the evidence for GDP and returns is mixed or suggestive. Novelty language such as 'breaks new ground' should be replaced by a bounded statement emphasizing the Greenbook archive, conditioning on accompanying point forecasts, comparison with private forecasts, and distributional evidence. Minor matters include harmonizing citations and checking all sample-date and data-provenance descriptions; the record reports potentially inconsistent Blue Chip dates, although one reviewer could not verify them from the supplied excerpt.

Defended critiques should be respected. The leakage concern should not be generalized into a claim that all estimates are invalid: the in-sample conditional associations remain informative, and no evidence establishes direct outcome-based dictionary tuning. Likewise, the paper need not add a causal identification design if it confines itself to predictive claims. Extensions involving richer language models, other forecasters, or additional mechanisms would be useful future work, not conditions for revision.

## OFFICIAL LETTER TO THE AUTHOR
Dear Authors,

The manuscript develops a useful measure of optimism and pessimism in Federal Reserve staff forecast narratives and asks whether that language contains information beyond the accompanying numerical forecasts. The strongest evidence supports a meaningful, bounded contribution: Greenbook narrative tone is conditionally associated with subsequent macroeconomic forecast errors, and the comparison with private forecasts and the analysis of adverse outcomes give the paper a coherent economic interpretation. The construction of the text variables is also documented more transparently than in many historical text applications.

We are inviting a major revision because the manuscript's prominent real-time interpretation is not yet supported by a genuinely prospective evaluation. The reported pseudo-out-of-sample exercise does not establish that all material feature choices were fixed before the evaluation period. In particular, the decay parameter defining Trend Tonality is discussed in relation to fit over the observed series, and no development/evaluation split is documented for the customized dictionary and preprocessing protocol. Full-sample standardization alone is not the central problem, and the early 40-document corpus may not directly affect an evaluation beginning later. The material issue is that the feature pipeline—especially the persistent Tonality transformation—was not demonstrably frozen or reconstructed recursively. Because Trend Tonality drives important results, this requires re-estimation rather than a verbal caveat.

Please designate an initial development sample, freeze the dictionary, preprocessing rules, smoothing choice, and forecasting specifications using that sample, and construct all subsequent inputs using only information available at each forecast origin. Recompute the pseudo-out-of-sample results under this protocol and distinguish them clearly from full-sample descriptive regressions. If the prospective results weaken, the paper can still make a valuable historical and in-sample contribution, but claims of material real-time forecasting or investment value must be revised accordingly.

Please also revisit inference for overlapping forecast horizons. The current description combines a 2k+1 lag rule with automatic Newey-West bandwidth selection, while forecast frequency changes from nearly monthly to eight times per year. Explain exactly which procedure is used and show that the principal conclusions survive bandwidths that cover the relevant calendar-time overlap, longer serial dependence, or estimates separated by frequency regime.

Several claims should be calibrated independently of those empirical repairs. First, the stock-return regressions demonstrate conditional predictability, not that investors failed to incorporate private cash-flow information. Present that mechanism as a possible interpretation alongside time-varying expected returns and omitted state variables. Second, describe downside concentration in an outcome-specific way: the evidence is strongest for unemployment and less uniform for GDP and stock returns. Third, narrow the novelty claim. The contribution is not textual sentiment analysis or narrative forecasting generally; it is the long Greenbook application, the comparison with accompanying Federal Reserve and Blue Chip point forecasts, and the distributional evidence. Please also audit citations, sample dates, and data provenance throughout, including any differing Blue Chip start dates.

A successful revision need not introduce a new causal design or adopt more elaborate language models. It must, however, separate credible historical associations from prospective claims, reconstruct the latter without future information, and provide inference appropriate to the data's timing and overlap. We view these as substantial but feasible revisions.

### Data_Scientist Analysis

**Structural Strength**: The manuscript provides unusually concrete documentation of its constructed variables: it identifies the Greenbook sections analyzed, lists the complete positive and negative dictionaries, describes the rolling tf-idf corpus, distinguishes Tonality from Trend Tonality and Tonality Shock, and names the real-time macroeconomic, Blue Chip, CRSP, and dividend-yield sources. This makes the main data pipeline substantially more auditable than is typical for historical text analysis.

**Domain Audit**: The paper's actual claim is that sentiment extracted from 1970–2009 Greenbook narratives contains incremental predictive information for macroeconomic forecast errors, policy-rate surprises, and stock returns, including information that could have benefited users in real time. The principal data-science problem is that the reported pseudo-out-of-sample evidence does not appear to reconstruct the text feature using only information available at each forecast origin. Tonality is standardized using the full sample, the smoothing parameter for Trend Tonality is described in Appendix B as being chosen to maximize fit, and the custom dictionary was fine-tuned on the economic-forecasting domain without a documented training/evaluation split. The first 40 documents also use a corpus containing future documents, although the stated out-of-sample evaluation begins after 64 observations and therefore partly mitigates that particular issue. Full-sample standardization alone is largely innocuous for linear regressions with intercepts, but ex post selection of the trend transformation and potentially the dictionary is feature-engineering leakage. Consequently, the reported out-of-sample R-squared statistics do not yet establish the claimed real-time forecasting or investment value. This does not invalidate the in-sample conditional associations, but it materially weakens the manuscript's stronger evidence about prospective usability. The audit is also impeded by inconsistent Blue Chip provenance: the main text and Table 9 imply a 1980–2009 sample, Appendix B says the forecasts run from 1992–2009, and Table 11 uses 1986–2009.

**Best-Case Contribution**: The strongest credible contribution is a new historical measurement exercise showing that the tone of internal Federal Reserve forecast narratives contains information about adverse macroeconomic outcomes beyond the accompanying point forecasts. The parallel patterns for Greenbook and Blue Chip forecast errors, together with the quantile results, provide suggestive evidence that narrative tone captures downside-risk information omitted from modal or otherwise incomplete point forecasts.

**Minimum Viable Revision**: Use the first 64 observations as an explicit training sample, freeze the dictionary and all text-processing choices there, estimate the tf-idf weights and normalization recursively, select the Trend Tonality decay parameter only in the training sample, and then recompute every reported out-of-sample statistic without future information. Report those results separately from the full-sample descriptive regressions. Also provide an observation-level data-lineage table containing document date, FOMC date, matched Blue Chip publication date, forecast horizon, outcome vintage, and exclusion reason, and reconcile the conflicting 1980, 1986, and 1992 Blue Chip start dates.

### Econometrician Analysis

**Structural Strength**: The empirical design is unusually well aligned with its main descriptive estimand: whether narrative tonality contains incremental predictive information conditional on the accompanying point forecast. The paper uses real-time vintages for realized macroeconomic variables, matches forecast horizons carefully, accounts for overlapping outcomes with HAC standard errors, reports pseudo-out-of-sample performance, and validates the pattern against Blue Chip rather than relying exclusively on an internal Federal Reserve forecasting relationship.

**Domain Audit**: The core evidence supports incremental prediction and association, not the stronger mechanism that Tonality represents private cash-flow information omitted from market prices. The macroeconomic regressions directly estimate whether Tonality improves on point forecasts, and their interpretation is appropriate. By contrast, stock-return predictability cannot by itself distinguish gradual incorporation of private information from omitted expected-return variation, other persistent state variables, or specification selection. Controlling for current unemployment and the dividend-price ratio helps but does not identify the information mechanism. The downside-risk conclusion should also be tempered: unemployment quantile results are strong, but direct equality tests are mixed for GDP and generally do not reject equality between median and lower-tail stock-return coefficients at conventional levels. Thus the defensible conclusion is that Tonality predicts forecast errors and returns, with suggestive and outcome-dependent evidence of stronger association in adverse tails.

**Best-Case Contribution**: The paper constructs a novel measure of sentiment from Federal Reserve staff forecast narratives and documents that its persistent component contains economically meaningful incremental information about future GDP growth, unemployment, monetary-policy forecast errors, and stock returns beyond contemporaneous numerical forecasts. The replication of macroeconomic predictability against Blue Chip forecast errors, together with the asymmetric unemployment results, makes the finding difficult to explain solely as an internal Greenbook point-forecast convention.

**Minimum Viable Revision**: Retain the predictive analysis but consistently describe the stock-return results as return predictability rather than evidence that prices fail to impound private cash-flow information. Temper the abstract and conclusion by describing downside asymmetry as strong for unemployment and suggestive or mixed for GDP and stock returns. Also clarify that any real-time interpretation is conditional on fixing or recursively constructing the dictionary, normalization, smoothing parameter, and forecasting specification without future information.
