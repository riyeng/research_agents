## EDITORIAL RATIONALE
Decision: MAJOR REVISION REQUIRED. The strongest credible version of the paper is an empirical study using proprietary 2001–2016 solicitation data to document a differential post-2009 decline in the co-movement of consumer-card offered rates with rivals’ lagged rate changes relative to small-business cards. The decrease-versus-increase asymmetry, stronger pattern in areas with more subprime borrowers, non-rate placebo outcomes, and alternative geographic and temporal aggregations make this a coherent and policy-relevant pattern. Novelty and insight are both best assessed as MEDIUM: the application and outcome are useful and appear new in this setting, while the data class and comparison strategy have precedents, and the proposed responsiveness statistic is not yet validated as a measure of competition.

The reviewers nevertheless agree that the current execution does not support the manuscript’s central causal interpretation. First, the treatment is a nationwide product-market intervention, rates are supplied by six national issuers, and the displayed specifications cluster only by county. County observations may repeat common issuer-time pricing decisions, while consumer-card-specific national shocks after 2009 may be confounded with the Act. The manuscript does not show that meaningful independent variation remains after national issuer-time movements or provide inference robust to the relevant cross-county dependence.

Second, Equation (2) estimates a change in a reaction slope involving a generated regressor, not a conventional difference-in-differences effect on outcome levels. Identification requires comparable counterfactual evolution of consumer- and small-business reaction slopes and comparable distributions and reliability of competitors’ rate changes. Changes in variance, persistence, issuer composition, or measurement error can alter the triple interaction without a change in strategic conduct. Recipient-covariate balance does not establish these conditions, and the limited pre-period dynamics reported do not establish stability over the full pre-Act period.

Third, the construction of annual changes and lagged competitor averages is insufficiently documented. The required consecutive-period joins, missing-cell rules, attrition, competitor weighting, and balanced-panel status are unclear. This is consequential because solicitation presence is an issuer choice that may respond to regulation. The support is also highly asymmetric: 823,174 consumer offers versus 28,625 small-business offers, and approximately 42,611 versus 1,898 issuer-county-year observations. The manuscript does not report cell-count distributions or quantify the reliability of the estimated cell averages. A county-level minimum-offer restriction does not ensure reliable issuer-card-type-county-year cells.

These are substantive flaws, not optional extensions, but no unresolved hard blocker makes revision infeasible. The existing data appear capable of supporting transparent data-lineage, stable-support, reliability, event-time, and dependence-aware analyses. The reviewers also defended the paper’s narrower contribution: even if stronger causal claims cannot be sustained, a carefully validated descriptive differential break would remain informative.

A separate substantial-rephrasal issue concerns the mapping from the estimates to competition and welfare. The manuscript provides mechanism-consistent intuition but no model, comparative static, or external validation establishing that the reaction coefficient is monotonic in welfare-relevant competition. Its own mean-reversion results underscore that the slope reflects broader pricing dynamics. Likewise, an offered rate minus the two-year Treasury yield is a spread, not a risk-adjusted economic markup, because expected losses, servicing costs, rewards, fees, and other attributes are not incorporated. The paper does not estimate consumer surplus or the Act’s net welfare effect. Unless stronger validation is supplied, the title, abstract, introduction, Section 5, and conclusion must describe reduced offered-rate responsiveness and increased Treasury spreads rather than established reductions in competition, higher economic markups, consumer harm, or net regulatory costs. No distinct minor-rephrasal or optional extension requests were identified; the remaining work is central to the paper’s soundness and interpretation.

## ROUND 1: INDEPENDENT AUDITS

### Econometrician Analysis

**Structural Strength**: The paper uses unusually rich proprietary solicitation data to study supply-side offer terms rather than equilibrium account outcomes, and it constructs a thoughtful set of within-framework checks: small-business cards as an untreated comparison, asymmetry between competitors' rate decreases and increases, timing tests, subprime-market heterogeneity, alternative geographic and temporal aggregation, and placebo outcomes for non-rate contract terms.

**Domain Audit**: The evidence credibly documents a differential post-2009 break in the lagged co-movement of offered consumer-card rates relative to small-business-card rates, especially following competitors' rate reductions. It does not yet credibly establish that the CARD Act caused a decline in competition. The treatment is a single nationwide product-market intervention, while the regressions treat thousands of county observations as independent identifying variation and cluster only by county. Rates are set by six national issuers and may be repeated across counties, so issuer-time and national product-market shocks can induce extensive cross-county dependence. More fundamentally, the proposed responsiveness coefficient is not uniquely a measure of strategic competition: a firm's rate change and lagged competitors' rate changes can co-move because of persistent common funding, macroeconomic, underwriting, or national pricing shocks. A post-2009 differential change in this coefficient can likewise reflect consumer and small-business portfolios responding differently to the financial crisis or other aggregate developments. Equations (1)-(5) include a post indicator and local controls but no comprehensive year-specific controls or design that isolates CARD Act exposure from consumer-card-specific aggregate shocks. The reported dynamic analysis only displays 2007 and 2008 as pre-period coefficients despite a 2001-2009 baseline, and its imprecise estimates do not establish stable parallel pre-treatment slope trends over the full pre-period. The non-rate placebo outcomes and asymmetric response results are suggestive of the proposed mechanism, but they do not solve the effective-treatment-cluster, common-shock, or measurement problems. Consequently, statements that the Act reduced competition and caused higher dispersion or markups exceed what the current identification and inference support.

**Best-Case Contribution**: The strongest defensible contribution is a novel empirical fact: in proprietary 2001-2016 mail-solicitation data, lagged offered-rate co-movement among major issuers fell markedly for consumer cards relative to small-business cards around implementation of the CARD Act. The break is concentrated in responses to rivals' rate reductions, is larger in counties with more subprime borrowers, does not appear for several non-rate offer terms, and coincides with increased offered-rate dispersion and spreads over Treasury yields. These patterns are consistent with, but do not uniquely identify, regulation-induced weakening of rate competition.

**Minimum Viable Revision**: Either recast the headline result as a descriptive differential break consistent with reduced competition, or materially strengthen identification and inference. At minimum, report a full event study of the consumer-versus-small-business responsiveness difference for every available pre- and post-period; absorb flexible year, issuer, geography, and relevant product-specific shocks where feasible; show how much independent rate variation remains after accounting for national issuer-time pricing; collapse or otherwise conduct inference at the level at which offer terms and the policy vary; and use inference robust to dependence across counties within issuer and year. The paper must explicitly distinguish the causal effect of the CARD Act from any consumer-card-specific post-2009 aggregate shock and validate, theoretically or empirically, when the proposed response coefficient is monotone in competition. If the existing national comparison cannot accomplish that, causal language in the title, abstract, introduction, Section 5, and conclusion should be narrowed.

### Data_Scientist Analysis

**Structural Strength**: The manuscript uses unusually granular proprietary solicitation data containing offered contract terms, recipient characteristics, geography, issuer identity, and survey weights. Constructing issuer-area-time measures from offers rather than relying on accepted accounts is a concrete strength because it moves measurement closer to lenders' supply decisions. The authors also compare county and MSA aggregation, annual and quarterly frequency, and several sample restrictions.

**Domain Audit**: The central data-construction concern is endogenous and insufficiently documented selection into issuer-area-time cells. The stated sample rules require only that a county receive offers in at least 10 of 16 years and that offers from at least two issuers be observed in at least one year. Yet the annual-change regressions require an issuer's cell in consecutive periods plus observable competitor cells in the lagged period. The manuscript does not report the exact joins, attrition, missing-cell rules, or whether the resulting issuer-card-type-county panel is balanced. This matters because solicitation presence and volume are themselves lender supply choices that the CARD Act may change, especially for small-business offers, of which only 28,625 are observed. If issuers enter, exit, or selectively target area-risk cells after the Act, the estimated change in responsiveness can reflect a changing set of observable cells rather than changed pricing behavior within stable competitive relationships. Recipient covariates do not correct selection on whether an offer cell exists. The robustness restriction to counties with at least three offers is not enough as described because it is unclear whether the threshold applies to each issuer-card-type-county-year cell, and it does not establish stable panel support. This is repairable within the existing data, but it currently places the central estimate in material doubt.

**Best-Case Contribution**: The strongest credible contribution is evidence from proprietary direct-mail solicitations that, among observed major-issuer geographic markets with sufficient solicitation activity, consumer-card offered rates became less likely than small-business-card rates to follow competitors' prior rate reductions after the CARD Act. The asymmetry between responses to decreases and increases, together with the absence of analogous changes for several non-rate terms, is consistent with repricing restrictions changing issuers' acquisition pricing incentives.

**Minimum Viable Revision**: Provide a reproducible cell-level data lineage: exact issuer list and harmonization, card-type labels, deduplication rules, survey-weight use, formulas for issuer and competitor averages, joins used to create changes and lags, treatment of missing cells, and attrition counts at every step. Then rerun the main and asymmetric-response specifications on stable issuer-area pairs observed in required consecutive periods on both sides of the Act, with a meaningful minimum number of offers per issuer-card-type-area-period; show weighted and unweighted estimates and sensitivity to leave-one-recipient-out or split-sample aggregation where feasible. **Structural Strength**: The paper maps its empirical tests closely to the legal design of the CARD Act. **Domain Audit**: The evidence supports a narrower policy claim than the headline assertion that the CARD Act reduced competition and harmed consumers.
