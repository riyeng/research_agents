The organizing mechanism is intuitive, and the broad bank panel offers scope for a useful historical contribution. ## EDITORIAL RATIONALE
Decision: MAJOR REVISION REQUIRED. The strongest credible version of the paper is a historical, bank-level study showing that, before 2008, lending responses associated with federal-funds-rate movements differed across banks with different securities and capital ratios, and that these differences were often larger when contemporaneous GDP growth was low. The analysis spans several loan categories, distinguishes liquidity-based and capitalization-based mechanisms, and examines market-share-weighted relationships. This is a useful extension of an established empirical framework. Novelty and insight are both best assessed as MEDIUM: explicit state dependence and the broader loan-category coverage are worthwhile, while the methods and data source are conventional and the causal interpretation remains unsettled.

The principal substantive flaw is identification. The manuscript interprets interactions between common policy movements and bank balance-sheet ratios as a bank credit-supply channel. Bank fixed effects remove time-invariant heterogeneity, quarter effects remove common shocks, and aggregate macroeconomic or SLOOS controls address aggregate conditions; none removes time-varying borrower composition, geography, sector exposure, or other bank-specific demand responses correlated with securities or capital ratios. The current evidence therefore supports heterogeneous reduced-form lending responses more securely than a causal bank credit-supply mechanism. A borrower-level or matched-market design would strengthen the supply interpretation, but it is not mandatory if the paper instead adopts the narrower estimand consistently.

A second substantive flaw concerns state timing. Equation (1) conditions four lagged policy changes on GDP growth realized in the outcome quarter. That GDP realization may follow and respond to some included policy changes and lending responses, so it does not cleanly measure the state prevailing when each policy innovation occurred. The prior-quarter GDP forecast does not resolve this concern: banks and borrowers can respond directly to expected growth, leaving the exclusion restriction undefended. The main analysis should use a state measured before the relevant policy innovations or otherwise provide a credible identification argument.

Inference also requires revision. The key policy and GDP variables vary at the quarterly aggregate level over a comparatively short time series, yet the reported inference clusters only by bank. The paper must use and justify procedures robust to common time shocks and serial dependence, potentially including two-way clustering where appropriate and methods suited to limited aggregate time variation. The significance of the headline interactions must be reassessed under those procedures.

The data record is not sufficiently auditable. The reported loan-growth extrema near plus or minus 50 suggest an undisclosed trimming or bounding rule; observation counts differ sharply across tables for apparently common outcomes; CRE is not consistently defined or summarized; merger adjustment, zero loan stocks, entry and exit, missing values, item mappings, and sample filters are not fully documented; the deposits-to-assets units appear inconsistent; and Table 4A reportedly repeats the same standard error across all displayed coefficients. These issues may affect samples, magnitudes, and significance and therefore require reconstruction rather than editorial clarification.

Market-share weighting presents both substantive and interpretive issues. The timing and formula for the weights must be disclosed and should be predetermined to avoid mechanical dependence on outcome-period loan stocks. Unless an aggregation identity is established, these regressions should be described as market-share-weighted bank-level relationships, not estimates of aggregate loan growth or a distinct large-bank channel.

A substantial rephrasal is independently necessary. All principal outcomes are bank loan-growth measures, so the paper does not establish that monetary policy is more effective at spurring economic activity, nor does it estimate output, employment, investment, consumption, welfare, or an aggregate multiplier. The linear specification also does not by itself establish that easing in weak conditions is more effective than tightening in strong conditions. Finally, the absence of consistent post-2007 evidence must be foregrounded, making clear that the contribution concerns a pre-crisis institutional regime. These are central claim-calibration issues, not judgments against the paper's underlying insight.

Defended critiques and extensions should be separated from requirements. A borrower-level design, welfare analysis, optimal policy rule, and operational policy prescription would be valuable extensions but are not required for a carefully delimited reduced-form paper. Likewise, a direct aggregate-lending estimand is unnecessary if the weighted regressions are transparently defined and interpreted narrowly. No unresolved blocker makes revision impossible, but the identification, timing, inference, and data-audit issues are substantive and prevent acceptance in the present form.

## OFFICIAL LETTER TO THE AUTHOR
Dear Authors,

The manuscript asks an important and well-focused question: whether bank-level monetary transmission varies with economic conditions. Its strongest contribution is evidence that, during 1986–2008, lending responses associated with federal-funds-rate movements differed across banks with different liquidity and capital positions and were often more pronounced when GDP growth was low. The distinction between securities-based and capitalization-based mechanisms, the coverage of several loan categories, and the explicit study of state dependence make the project promising.

Our decision is MAJOR REVISION REQUIRED. The paper is not yet ready for acceptance because several central empirical claims exceed what the current design establishes, and the data and inference require a more complete audit.

First, the design does not presently separate bank credit supply from heterogeneous credit demand. Bank and quarter fixed effects and aggregate macroeconomic or SLOOS controls do not absorb time-varying differences in borrower composition, geography, or sector exposure correlated with banks' securities and capital ratios. You should either introduce credible bank-specific demand controls or a finer borrower-market comparison, or consistently recast the estimand as a heterogeneous reduced-form bank-lending response. The latter is a viable revision path and does not require an entirely new borrower-level project, but it would require substantial changes to the abstract, introduction, empirical interpretation, and conclusion. The mixed results using Romer-Romer innovations, especially the lack of consistent support for the balance-sheet mechanism, should be made central to the assessment rather than treated as a secondary robustness exercise.

Second, contemporaneous GDP growth is problematic as the conditioning state. It is realized after at least some of the four lagged policy changes and may itself respond to policy and lending. Please redesign the main specification around a state measured before the relevant policy innovations, such as an appropriately lagged growth measure or pre-policy output gap, and explain the timing precisely. The prior-quarter GDP forecast is not a valid instrument merely because it predates realized GDP: expected growth can directly affect current borrowing and lending. Retain that strategy only if you can defend an exclusion restriction; otherwise remove it as an identification claim.

Third, reassess inference. Because the policy and state variables are common quarterly series, clustering only by bank may understate uncertainty. Report inference robust to common time shocks and serial dependence, with a clear justification suited to the limited number of quarters. The headline conclusions must be evaluated using those revised standard errors.

Fourth, provide a complete and reproducible account of data construction. Please identify Call Report items and reporting-break crosswalks; explain merger adjustment; disclose all trimming, winsorization, and balance-sheet screens; state how zero loan stocks, entry, exit, missing values, and joins are handled; define every loan category consistently; and reconcile observation counts across all tables. In particular, explain the loan-growth extrema near plus or minus 50, the difference between the roughly 523,000 and 821,000 total-lending counts, and the CRE counts and definitions. Correct the deposits-to-assets unit inconsistency and regenerate and verify Table 4A, where identical standard errors across columns indicate a possible table-production error. Rerun the headline estimates on transparent common samples and report sensitivity to outcome trimming or bounding.

Fifth, define the market-share weights exactly, including their timing and formula. Use predetermined weights or justify any alternative. Unless you derive an aggregation identity, interpret these estimates only as market-share-weighted bank-level relationships, not as aggregate lending responses or direct evidence of a separate large-bank channel.

Finally, revise the central policy language. The paper estimates bank loan growth, not GDP, employment, investment, consumption, or welfare. It therefore cannot conclude that monetary policy is more effective at spurring economic activity. Nor does the linear state interaction alone establish an easing-versus-tightening asymmetry. The defensible claim concerns stronger lending sensitivity in low-growth observations, subject to the revised identification. The abstract and conclusion should also foreground that the channel is not consistently detected after 2007 and limit contemporary implications accordingly.

These concerns are serious but potentially repairable. A successful revision need not add welfare analysis, an optimal policy rule, or a borrower-level design if the paper adopts a disciplined reduced-form interpretation. It must, however, deliver transparent data construction, credible timing, dependence-robust inference, and claims that match the estimated outcomes.

Sincerely,

## ROUND 1: INDEPENDENT AUDITS

### Econometrician Analysis

**Structural Strength**: The paper uses a large quarterly bank panel, examines several economically distinct loan categories, separates liquidity-based and capitalization-based transmission mechanisms, and tests whether their estimated strength varies with aggregate activity. The inclusion of bank and quarter fixed effects in the preferred specifications is particularly useful for removing time-invariant bank heterogeneity and shocks common to all banks.

**Domain Audit**: The strongest defensible estimand is a heterogeneous reduced-form association: before 2008, loan growth at banks with different securities or capital ratios responded differently to federal-funds-rate movements, and that differential varied with contemporaneous GDP growth. The design does not by itself identify the bank credit-supply channel. Identification requires banks with different ratios to have the same policy- and cycle-sensitive loan demand after controls, but securities and capital ratios can be correlated with business models, borrower composition, geography, and sector exposure. Bank fixed effects remove permanent level differences, while quarter fixed effects remove only common demand shocks; neither removes differential demand responses correlated with the ratios. Aggregate SLOOS controls likewise cannot absorb bank-specific borrower demand. There are additional concerns reinforcing this limitation: the state variable is contemporaneous GDP growth and can itself respond to monetary policy and credit; the proposed instrument, a lagged GDP forecast, may predict anticipated credit demand and therefore lacks a defended exclusion restriction; and the more plausibly exogenous Romer-Romer shock exercise supports the securities-based results but, by the paper's own account, not the balance-sheet channel. Inference is also potentially too optimistic because standard errors are clustered only by bank even though monetary-policy and GDP variation is common across banks and comes from a relatively short quarterly time series. Market-share-weighted regressions estimate a weighted bank-level relationship, not directly an aggregate lending response or a separate large-bank effect. Finally, no real-activity outcome is estimated, so the claim that policy is more effective at 'spurring economic activity' is not established by the lending regressions.

**Best-Case Contribution**: The paper documents a broad and economically interesting pre-2008 pattern: conditional differences in bank loan growth associated with monetary-policy movements were generally larger when GDP growth was low, especially for total, residential, and consumer lending, and some capitalization-related patterns remain when observations are weighted toward banks with larger market shares. This extends prior bank-lending-channel evidence across loan categories and introduces business-cycle state dependence as a central empirical dimension.

**Minimum Viable Revision**: Use a predetermined economic state, such as lagged GDP growth or a pre-policy output gap, and an externally identified monetary-policy shock in the main specifications; report inference robust to common time shocks, such as two-way clustering or appropriate time-series dependence corrections; and directly address differential credit demand with bank-specific demand exposures or a design offering finer borrower-market comparisons. If such demand separation is infeasible in the current data, recast the results as state-dependent heterogeneous lending associations rather than identification of a causal credit-supply channel, and remove the claim that the regressions show effects on real economic activity. The mixed Romer-Romer results should be made central rather than treated as a secondary robustness check.

### Data_Scientist Analysis

**Structural Strength**: The paper assembles a long quarterly bank panel, analyzes several loan categories, and consistently relates monetary-policy exposure to predetermined bank balance-sheet measures. The use of bank fixed effects, time fixed effects, lagged balance-sheet ratios, macroeconomic controls, SLOOS demand and standards measures, and alternative monetary-policy and GDP measures is a strong empirical structure in principle.

**Domain Audit**: The actual claim is that, among U.S. commercial banks from 1986 to 2008, the bank credit channel was stronger when real GDP growth was low, across more loan categories and a broader range of banks than previously documented. However, the manuscript does not provide a coherent, auditable account of the dataset used to establish this result. Several apparently consequential transformations and sample changes are omitted or internally inconsistent. Table 1 defines loan growth simply as the log change multiplied by 100, yet every loan-growth variable has extrema almost exactly at -50 and 50, strongly indicating undisclosed trimming or winsorization. The same table reports 523,517 observations for total lending, while Table 2 reports 821,146 observations for regressions whose dependent variable is purportedly the same total-lending flow. CRE regressions reach 823,290 observations in Table 3, although Table 1 neither defines nor summarizes CRE lending and instead lists an unexplained 'Other categories' variable. The manuscript also does not document Call Report item mappings across reporting changes, merger adjustment, treatment of zero loan stocks before taking logarithms, entry and exit, missing-data rules, or whether weighted regressions use contemporaneous or lagged loan-market shares. Contemporaneous loan-stock shares would incorporate the outcome-period loan stock into regression weights and would not be equivalent to aggregate lending growth. These issues prevent a reader from determining whether results across specifications arise from the stated economic interactions or from undocumented transformations and changing samples.
