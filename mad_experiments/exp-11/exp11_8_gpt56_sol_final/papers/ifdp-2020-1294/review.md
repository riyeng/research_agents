## CONSTRUCTIVE SUMMARY
The manuscript is promising because it gives readers a coherent guide to measures that are often discussed as though they were interchangeable. Its comparative treatment of source, horizon, information set, and real-time availability is useful, and the underlying survey architecture is sound. The revision should protect that contribution by making the inferential status of the reviewed evidence transparent. The central problem is not that the cited findings are necessarily wrong, but that heterogeneous descriptive, predictive, VAR-based, and structural results are currently synthesized as a general causal conclusion. Correcting that overclaim, adding an evidence-type map, improving bounded provenance disclosure, and tightening scope and historical framing would provide a credible path to a substantially stronger manuscript.

## EDITORIAL RATIONALE
Decision: MAJOR REVISION REQUIRED. The strongest credible version of the manuscript is a broad, policy-oriented survey that makes a fragmented literature more navigable. Its principal contribution is comparative synthesis: it explains how prominent measures differ by source, statistical object, horizon, information set, and real-time availability, and it illustrates why measures such as EPU, the VIX, forecast-based uncertainty, and ambiguity should not be treated as interchangeable. The real-time versus ex-post discussion is particularly promising. Novelty and insight are both medium: the unified presentation is useful and non-trivial, but the paper does not introduce a new estimator, identification strategy, or major empirical fact, and much of the exposition remains measure-by-measure rather than developing a systematic cross-cutting framework.

The decisive issue is a substantial-rephrasal problem, not a rejection-level failure of the underlying survey. The abstract and introduction state that uncertainty has significant or first-order real and financial effects and that their size and persistence depend crucially on uncertainty's source. The evidence reviewed, however, includes contemporaneous associations, forecasting exercises, quantile-regression predictive distributions, bivariate VAR responses, and structural or stochastic-volatility model results. These approaches do not estimate a common causal object or apply a uniform identification standard. Cross-study differences may reflect measurement, horizon, information timing, sample, specification, and identifying assumptions as well as the source of uncertainty. The manuscript therefore supports a more qualified synthesis: many uncertainty measures are associated with or predictive of real and financial outcomes, while causal estimates are study- and identification-dependent. Because the stronger claim appears in the abstract and principal lessons, local edits would be insufficient; the inferential hierarchy must be made explicit throughout.

A second concern involves reproducibility and provenance. The initial audit identified incomplete documentation for some staff-calculated and vendor-based series, including identifiers, vintages, forecast specifications, market-contract rules, interpolation choices, calendar alignment, and missing-data treatment. Later debate appropriately qualified the severity because some of the strongest examples were not verifiable in the supplied excerpt and no central result was shown to reverse under alternative conventions. Accordingly, the record does not establish an argument-damaging measurement failure. Still, basic provenance is a defended and bounded revision: each displayed series should be labeled as downloaded unchanged, reconstructed from a cited method, or newly calculated, with retrieval vintage, sample, and displayed transformations. For genuinely reconstructed or author-calculated series, material implementation choices should be documented. A complete executable archive would be valuable future-facing transparency, but the record does not justify making an exhaustive archive a separate publication barrier.

Minor rephrasal issues should also be addressed. The survey should qualify claims of being comprehensive or exhaustive, state its inclusion criteria, and acknowledge omitted or selectively treated families. The taxonomy should clarify that news, surveys, econometric methods, and asset markets describe sources or construction approaches, whereas Knightian uncertainty or ambiguity is a different conceptual dimension. Historical wording should distinguish antecedent work on realized return variation and quadratic variation from Andersen, Bollerslev, Diebold, and Labys's high-frequency implementation, and modern ambiguity models should be described as formalizations related to rather than historically synonymous with Knight's distinction.

The critiques that are not sustained as major barriers should remain proportionate. The record contains no unresolved blocker and no evidence that the taxonomy or individual measure descriptions are generally unsound. Nor is a new dataset, wholesale re-estimation, latent-factor decomposition, or comprehensive replication platform required for a viable revision. Such work could deepen the paper in the future, especially by mapping common and distinct components across measures, but it is an extension rather than a condition of the present decision.

The headline conclusion should state that uncertainty measures are often associated with or predictive of real and financial outcomes, while causal magnitudes and source-specific differences remain identification-dependent. The record does not establish that a full replication archive is necessary to validate the survey, but the manuscript should not claim detailed replicability beyond what its documentation supports. Likewise, present modern ambiguity models as formal developments related to, rather than exact synonyms for, Knight's historical risk-versus-uncertainty distinction.

These revisions should preserve the paper's strongest contribution while making its conclusions commensurate with the evidence. We do not request a new empirical project or wholesale re-estimation. We do require a systematic recalibration of the central claims and a clearer audit trail for the paper's own comparative displays.

## ROUND 1: INDEPENDENT AUDITS

### Historian Analysis

**Structural Strength**: The manuscript consistently traces each uncertainty measure to named originators, explains its construction, displays its historical behavior, and summarizes associated findings. Table A.1 is especially useful because it links measures, outcomes, methodologies, sources, and figures in one place.

**Domain Audit**: The literature synthesis is broad and mostly careful, but several lineage and scope claims require qualification. Most importantly, the conclusion states that the list of measures is believed to be "exhaustive," even though the manuscript itself treats some substantial families only briefly, including forecast disagreement and firm-level survey measures, and defines its organizing categories selectively. The statement that realized volatility was "pioneered by Andersen, Bollerslev, Diebold, and Labys (2001, 2003)" should recognize antecedent work on realized return variation and quadratic variation, while identifying Andersen et al.'s more specific contribution involving high-frequency realized-volatility measurement. Likewise, equating "Knightian uncertainty" with modern ambiguity aversion is a useful convention but not a historically exact attribution: Knight's risk-versus-uncertainty distinction should be separated from the later formal multiple-prior and smooth-ambiguity literatures. These are literature-framing issues rather than defects in the reported constructions or evidence.

**Best-Case Contribution**: The strongest credible contribution is a wide-ranging, policy-oriented taxonomy that places news-based, survey-based, econometric, market-implied, and ambiguity-related measures in a common framework; explains what each measure captures and at what horizon; and compares their behavior around major episodes, including COVID-19. Its value lies in synthesis, comparability, and guidance about measurement choice rather than in introducing a new estimator or identifying a new causal effect.

**Minimum Viable Revision**: Replace "exhaustive" with "broad" or "selective but comprehensive within the stated categories"; state explicit inclusion criteria and acknowledge omitted measure families; refine the realized-volatility lineage to distinguish antecedent quadratic-variation work from Andersen et al.'s high-frequency implementation; and describe ambiguity aversion as a modern formalization related to, rather than synonymous with, Knight's historical distinction. The abstract and conclusion should also make clear that claims about economic effects synthesize cited studies rather than constitute new causal estimates by this paper.

### Data_Scientist Analysis

**Structural Strength**: The manuscript imposes a useful common structure on a highly heterogeneous measurement literature. For most measures it identifies the underlying outcome, broad data source, construction idea, horizon, and interpretation; Table A.1 then links each measure to its type, source, and figures. The discussion of real-time versus ex-post macroeconomic uncertainty is especially strong because it explicitly shows that data vintage can materially alter both the measured series and its forecasting performance.

**Domain Audit**: The central data-provenance weakness is that the comparative figures and several Federal Reserve staff-calculated series are not reproducible from the manuscript. Source labels such as "Bloomberg Finance LP," "ICAP," "Reuters," "Website," and "Federal Reserve Board's staff calculations" do not specify exact series identifiers, download dates or vintages, contract filters, adjustment conventions, interpolation settings, sample inclusion rules, trading-calendar alignment, or missing-value treatment. This is material for cross-country realized volatility, option-implied volatility, variance-risk-premium, swaption, currency, and oil-option figures. For example, Figure 17 defines realized volatility as the square root of the sum of daily squared returns over 22 trading days, but does not identify the index tickers, return convention, dividend treatment, currency convention, holiday alignment, or rule when fewer than 22 valid observations are available. Figure 23 additionally depends on an in-sample forecast using several regressors, but provides no estimated specification, rolling versus full-sample convention, or treatment of unavailable option observations. The option-distribution sections describe conceptual choices but omit enough implementation detail that different reasonable interpolation, extrapolation, quote-cleaning, and maturity-selection choices could change tail probabilities and insurance costs. Because the paper explicitly claims to explain calculations "in detail" to facilitate replicability and uses the resulting plots to compare behavior around crises, this is a defect in the supporting evidence rather than merely a request for broader robustness. It is repairable without changing the paper's survey framework.

**Best-Case Contribution**: The strongest credible contribution is a broad, policy-oriented taxonomy that allows readers to compare what prominent uncertainty measures actually capture, how their information sets and horizons differ, and why measures such as the VIX, economic-policy uncertainty, macroeconomic forecast uncertainty, and Knightian ambiguity need not move together. The integration of news, surveys, econometric forecasts, asset derivatives, and ambiguity measures in one survey, together with early COVID-19 illustrations and explicit attention to real-time measurement, is a useful bounded synthesis rather than a new estimator or causal study.

**Minimum Viable Revision**: Add a frozen replication archive for every figure and staff-calculated series, or provide an equivalent immutable data appendix. It should list exact vendor and public-series identifiers, extraction dates and vintages, sample dates, units, return and price conventions, contract and liquidity filters, quote-cleaning rules, maturity and strike interpolation or extrapolation algorithms, calendar joins, missing-data rules, normalization windows, moving averages, forecast specifications, and all code needed to generate the displayed series. Table A.1 should distinguish series downloaded unchanged from series reconstructed by the authors and series newly calculated from raw inputs. If proprietary licenses prevent redistribution, release transformed figure data and executable code with precise vendor-field mappings.
