## EDITORIAL RATIONALE
Decision: MAJOR REVISION REQUIRED. The strongest credible version of the paper establishes a potentially useful U.S. predictive regularity: measures of strong real activity appear to improve forecasts of the NFCI nonfinancial-leverage subindex, with economically meaningful reductions in RMSFE. The combination of historical narrative, recursive forecasting, and structural analysis is promising, and the paper appropriately investigates a direction of association that receives less attention than financial amplification of downturns. Novelty and insight are both best assessed as MEDIUM: the empirical package is distinctive and policy relevant, but it uses established data and methods, does not yet sharply distinguish itself from adjacent business-financial-cycle research, and does not discriminate among the proposed mechanisms.

The reviewers nevertheless agree that the present execution does not support several central claims. First, Tables 3 and 4 compare recursively estimated bivariate VARs with nested AR benchmarks using Diebold-Mariano tests. The manuscript does not explain how inference addresses nesting, recursive parameter estimation, or serial correlation from overlapping four- and eight-quarter forecast errors. The RMSFE ratios remain informative descriptive evidence, but the reported p-values and declarations of robust statistical superiority are not established by the stated procedure. Second, the structural conclusions cannot be adequately audited from the supplied material. The visible manuscript ends while introducing the sign restrictions and does not supply the impulse responses, admissible-draw diagnostics, historical decompositions, or uncertainty across admissible rotations needed to support claims about structural effects and the relative importance of business-cycle versus monetary-policy shocks. The audit record also indicates concern about drawing relative-importance conclusions from a representative median-target decomposition without uncertainty across the identified set. These are substantive flaws, not matters of presentation.

Additional substantive issues concern interpretation and design. Positive movements in NFCI, ANFCI, risk, credit, leverage, and nonfinancial leverage cannot automatically receive the same interpretation as accumulation of financial imbalances; the manuscript itself notes that higher risk, tighter credit conditions, and declining leverage correspond to tighter financial conditions. The direction and economic meaning of each index must therefore be established separately. The pseudo-out-of-sample exercise uses one-sided transformations, but the manuscript does not document vintage-by-vintage macroeconomic and NFCI data or release timing; absent that documentation, it should be described as pseudo-out-of-sample rather than genuine real-time forecasting. The conditional forecasts are illustrative because they assume advance knowledge of the output-gap path, focus on two selected expansion endpoints, and lack a prespecified episode rule and formal benchmark comparison.

The historical evidence also requires substantive reconstruction. The manuscript alternates among three propositions: vulnerabilities accumulate during expansions, disturbances begin while the economy is hot, and disturbances follow earlier expansions. These are not equivalent. The claim that almost all disturbances begin during hot periods is not supported consistently by the displayed chronology: the table includes long leads, mismatched groupings, and foreign crises without an evident contemporaneous U.S. hot period. Moreover, Figure 1 presents selected events while Table 1 is called comprehensive, yet no reproducible event-universe, uniform dating source, coding protocol, or treatment of exceptions is provided. The defensible historical proposition may be about vulnerability buildup during or after strong expansions rather than crisis onset during them, but that must be determined through transparent coding rather than assertion. This is also a substantial-rephrasal issue because the abstract and introduction currently state the stronger onset proposition.

Minor rephrasal issues concern policy. The evidence may justify treating strong activity as one input into financial-stability surveillance, alongside direct vulnerability measures. It does not identify an overheating threshold, welfare loss, treatment effect, intervention rule, or preferred monetary or macroprudential instrument. References to an economy running 'too hot' and to countercyclical-capital-buffer decisions should therefore be qualified. This policy calibration alone would not warrant major revision, but it is necessary alongside the substantive repairs.

Defended critiques should also guide revision. The record does not establish that prior literature duplicates the paper's exact recursive forecasting exercise for NFCI nonfinancial leverage, so the contribution should not be dismissed as non-novel. At the same time, the claim to fill a broad empirical gap is insufficiently demonstrated. A direct comparison with the closest cited work should identify differences in direction of prediction, outcome variable, estimand, and empirical design. No unrelated extensions are required. The revision should concentrate on validating the existing forecasting evidence, making the structural results auditable, reconstructing the historical exercise, and aligning all claims with what those analyses establish.

## OFFICIAL LETTER TO THE AUTHOR
Dear Authors,

The paper asks an important and coherent question: whether strong macroeconomic performance precedes the accumulation of financial vulnerabilities. Its most promising result is the apparent predictive relationship between measures of strong activity and subsequent nonfinancial leverage. The reported RMSFE reductions are economically meaningful, and the effort to triangulate historical evidence, recursive forecasts, and a structural VAR gives the project a potentially valuable scope.

After considering the manuscript and the reviewer record, however, I am requesting a major revision. The central predictive, structural, and historical claims are not yet supported at the level required for publication.

The first priority is forecast inference. Tables 3 and 4 compare a bivariate VAR with an AR benchmark that is nested within it, using recursively estimated forecasts at horizons of one, four, and eight quarters. The manuscript reports Diebold-Mariano p-values but does not explain how the procedure handles the nested-model null, parameter estimation, or overlapping multi-step forecast errors. Please re-establish inference using a procedure appropriate to these features and report the statistic, covariance or long-run-variance estimator, and finite-sample implementation clearly. If the RMSFE gains remain economically large but are not statistically robust under valid inference, the abstract, tables, and conclusions must say so.

Second, the structural claims require complete and auditable evidence. The supplied manuscript material ends while introducing the sign restrictions, although the abstract and introduction claim that business-cycle shocks are important drivers of leverage and may be more important than monetary-policy shocks. A revision must report the impulse responses, admissible-draw diagnostics, historical decompositions, and uncertainty across admissible rotations. In particular, relative-importance claims should not rest on a single median-target decomposition. Please show uncertainty for leverage responses and historical shock contributions across the identified set, using appropriately informative credible sets or probabilities. If the ranking of business-cycle and monetary-policy contributions is not robust, recast the exercise as suggestive and model conditional.

Third, clarify what each NFCI measure represents. The manuscript currently interprets positive responses of the aggregate indexes and all subindexes as expansions of financial imbalances, even though some components measure tighter credit conditions or realized financial stress and the stated sign convention for leverage differs. Define the direction and economic content of each series separately, and distinguish ex ante vulnerability from subsequent tightening or stress. The especially credible result appears to concern nonfinancial leverage; the paper should not generalize that interpretation to heterogeneous indexes without support.

Fourth, reconstruct the historical analysis around one clearly defined temporal proposition. Vulnerability buildup during an expansion, disturbance onset during an expansion, and a disturbance following an earlier expansion are distinct claims. Establish an ex ante event-selection rule, provide uniform dating sources, code every event in the stated universe at onset and at fixed leads, and disclose exceptions. Figure 1 and Table 1 should cover the same event universe or clearly explain why they differ. The current claim that almost all disturbances begin while the economy is hot is inconsistent with several dates in the table and with the manuscript's acknowledgment that buildup is not precisely dated. The abstract and introduction should state only the proposition supported by the reconstructed evidence.

Please also clarify the forecasting design. One-sided transformations do not by themselves establish genuine real-time performance. Document whether GDP, unemployment, NFCI, and slack measures were reconstructed from vintages and release calendars available at each forecast origin. If current-vintage data were used, consistently call the exercise pseudo-out-of-sample. The conditional forecasts should either be formalized with a prespecified episode rule, suitable benchmarks, and forecast-loss comparisons, or presented explicitly as illustrations that condition on future output-gap information rather than as independent evidence of hot-period forecasting performance.

The policy implications require narrower wording. The results may support using strong activity as one surveillance indicator alongside direct measures of vulnerability. They do not establish that policymakers should restrain an expansion, activate the countercyclical capital buffer, or choose monetary rather than macroprudential intervention. Please remove language implying an estimated 'too hot' threshold and state that the paper does not evaluate welfare, intervention effects, timing, or instrument choice.

Finally, sharpen the novelty claim. The record supports a bounded, medium-level contribution rather than the assertion that the paper fills a broadly open gap. Compare the paper directly with the closest cited research on business-financial-cycle interactions, credit booms, and credit sentiment, explaining what differs in the direction of prediction, outcome, estimand, and empirical package.

A successful revision need not abandon the current data or framework. It must, however, validate the forecast comparisons, expose uncertainty in the structural exercise, make the historical evidence reproducible, and align the causal, historical, and policy claims with the resulting evidence. I would be willing to consider a substantially revised manuscript addressing these points.

### Econometrician Analysis

**Structural Strength**: The paper triangulates the same hypothesis with historical narratives, recursive pseudo-out-of-sample forecasts, and a structurally identified VAR. In particular, benchmarking bivariate forecasts against an autoregressive model and replacing two-sided slack estimates with one-sided measures are sensible attempts to distinguish in-sample comovement from usable predictive content.

**Domain Audit**: The manuscript's defensible core is a U.S. time-series prediction claim: strong macroeconomic conditions precede increases in the NFCI nonfinancial-leverage subindex. Its stronger causal and relative-driver conclusions are not yet supported by the reported inference. First, Tables 3 and 4 compare a larger VAR with a nested AR using ordinary Diebold-Mariano tests under recursive estimation; the manuscript does not address the nonstandard null distribution created by nested forecast models or explain whether inference at four- and eight-quarter horizons accounts for overlapping forecast errors. Thus, the bold significance declarations do not follow from the stated procedure, although the large RMSFE reductions may survive valid inference. Second, the SVAR is set-identified by impact sign restrictions, with the only narrative restriction concerning a tightening monetary-policy shock in 1979Q4. Nonfinancial leverage is unrestricted for every shock. The impulse-response evidence is presented with 68 percent credible sets, while the historical decomposition underlying the conclusion that business-cycle shocks are often more important than monetary-policy shocks is computed from a single median-target draw. No uncertainty over admissible rotations or historical shock contributions is reported. Consequently, the model demonstrates that some admissible sign-restricted decompositions generate the reported leverage responses, but it does not yet establish that the relative importance of business-cycle shocks is robust across the identified set.

**Best-Case Contribution**: The strongest credible contribution is a new and policy-relevant empirical regularity for the United States since the 1970s: several measures of strong activity predict subsequent nonfinancial leverage, with economically sizable pseudo-out-of-sample forecast improvements that are more consistent across horizons than those obtained from common financial spreads. The structural exercise provides suggestive, model-conditional evidence that both macroeconomic and monetary-policy shocks can contribute to leverage dynamics.

**Minimum Viable Revision**: Retain the present data and framework, but re-establish forecast significance using inference valid for nested recursively estimated models and overlapping multi-step errors. For the structural results, report posterior or identified-set uncertainty for impulse responses and historical decompositions across all admissible rotations, including probabilities or 90/95 percent credible sets for leverage responses and for business-cycle contributions exceeding monetary-policy contributions. If those results are not robust, narrow the causal conclusion to predictive evidence plus suggestive model-conditional decompositions.
