## EDITORIAL RATIONALE
Decision: MAJOR REVISION REQUIRED. The strongest credible version of the paper is a transparent quantitative stress test of a specific benchmark: candidate factor t-statistics are independent, correctly calibrated standard-normal null draws, and publication depends only on the realized t-statistic bin. The manuscript's distinctive contribution is to map the unusually heavy right tail of selected asset-pricing statistics into relative publication weights and then into an intuitive measure of required search effort. This is a promising and memorable diagnostic. The assessments place both novelty and insight at MEDIUM overall: the histogram-to-search-effort translation is useful, but it is neither a new general identification method nor based on a new directly observed dataset.

The principal substantive flaw is scale nonidentification. If q_i = Phi(e_{i+1}) - Phi(e_i), Equation (3) is f_i = p_i q_i / sum_j p_j q_j and is invariant to replacing every p_i by c p_i. The observed histogram therefore identifies relative publication weights, not their absolute scale. The restriction sum_i p_i = 1 is not justified because the p_i are conditional publication probabilities for different bins, not mutually exclusive probability masses. Consequently, the manuscript cannot call the model exactly identified or treat Equations (6)-(8) and Table 2 as point estimates. This issue was independently identified by all three reviewers and remains unresolved. It is serious because the absolute publication rate drives the headline researcher-years calculation, but it is repairable. Writing p_i = c(f_i/q_i), feasibility requires p_i <= 1 and hence c <= 1/max_i(f_i/q_i). Since the modeled f_i sum to one, the maximum feasible unconditional publication rate is c_max = min_i(q_i/f_i). This yields an upper bound on publication and a lower bound on search time. The reported CZ maximum probability of 0.99977 suggests that the corrected CZ result may be numerically close to the current calculation, but this must be derived for both datasets rather than presumed.

A second substantive concern is tail specification. The extreme magnitudes arise from standard-normal probabilities as small as approximately 10^-12. The manuscript's central-limit argument and one portfolio illustration do not establish accurate normal calibration at t-statistics between six and eight across heterogeneous searches. No sensitivity analysis addresses t distributions, overdispersed normals, variance mixtures, empirical nulls, or other plausible tail specifications. Because modest tail changes could materially alter the implied search volume, either the paper must remain explicitly a benchmark result or it must demonstrate robustness under plausible alternatives.

A third substantive concern concerns the empirical inputs. The CZ series consists of reconstructed equal-weighted long-short portfolio statistics rather than necessarily the originally reported statistics, while the HLZ series is simulated from reported fitted parameters rather than observed raw data. The manuscript does not provide the simulation size, seed, exact parameter values, Monte Carlo uncertainty, sensitivity to alternative parameter rows, or evidence that the fitted model preserves the decisive upper-tail bins. These limitations do not invalidate a carefully labeled benchmark exercise, but they currently prevent full auditability and make uncertainty in the tail moments especially important.

The central substantial-rephrasal issue is that the manuscript generalizes beyond its model. Equations (1)-(2) address selection among independent, correctly calibrated standard-normal null statistics with publication determined by the statistic's bin. They do not encompass adaptive specification searches, misspecified standard errors, heavy-tailed or otherwise nonstandard null statistics, or richer mixtures of true and false factors. Claims that the exercise disproves p-hacking, establishes that publication bias is relatively minor, or refutes a unified literature would materially mislead readers. The cited papers also concern heterogeneous mechanisms, and the manuscript does not show that each endorses the exact all-null benchmark tested here. The defensible claim is narrower: under this pure normal-null, t-statistic-only selection model, the observed tail implies an extremely demanding amount of search, even under the fastest feasible publication process if the corrected bound remains large.

Minor rephrasal and presentation issues include inconsistent arithmetic. Section 3.2 reports 528 million years where Table 2 reports 277,524,922 years, and approximately 15,000 years where the corresponding table entry is 7,709 years. The table appears internally consistent with the stated HLZ publication rate, while the prose may mix datasets or targets. Every numerical statement must be regenerated from a common calculation and labeled by dataset.

Defended critiques and proportionality matter for the decision. There are no unresolved hard blockers, and the reviewers agree that the identification problem can likely be repaired within the existing framework by converting point estimates into sharp bounds. A comprehensive new historical study is not required if the paper accurately positions itself as a stress test and avoids attributing its exact benchmark uniformly to prior work. Likewise, broader distributional analysis is an extension if the claims are narrowed, but it becomes necessary if the paper retains general claims about p-hacking. The appropriate grade is therefore major revision rather than rejection: the core idea remains promising, but the current estimand, interpretation, empirical documentation, and headline claims require substantive correction.

## OFFICIAL LETTER TO THE AUTHOR
Dear Author,

The paper has a strong organizing idea: it turns an informal account of selective publication into an explicit model and translates the right tail of published factor t-statistics into an intuitive measure of required search effort. In its strongest form, the paper can provide a useful stress test of a pure-noise benchmark in which candidate statistics are independent, correctly calibrated standard-normal null draws and publication depends on the realized statistic. The reviewers found this contribution promising and potentially memorable.

I am inviting a major revision because the headline quantitative result is not identified as currently presented. Equation (3) is unchanged if all bin-specific publication probabilities are multiplied by a common positive constant. The published histogram therefore identifies their relative values but not their absolute scale. The restriction that these probabilities sum to one is not implied by their definition: they are conditional probabilities of publication in different t-statistic bins, not probability masses over mutually exclusive outcomes. Thus the model is not exactly identified in the sense claimed, and the unconditional publication probabilities and researcher-years in Equations (6)-(8) and Table 2 do not follow as point estimates.

There is, however, a constructive repair. Parameterize p_i = c r_i, where the histogram identifies relative weights r_i proportional to f_i/q_i and q_i is the null probability of bin i. Then impose the probability restriction p_i <= 1. The largest feasible scale is c_max = 1/max_i r_i, equivalently min_i(q_i/f_i) under the natural normalization. This gives the maximum publication rate compatible with the histogram and therefore the minimum required search time. Please derive this result formally, recompute both applications, and present the output as bounds rather than point estimates. The reported CZ maximum publication probability near one suggests that its numerical result may change little, but the paper must establish this explicitly for both CZ and HLZ.

The revised paper must also confront the dependence of the result on extreme standard-normal tail probabilities. The current central-limit justification does not establish accurate calibration at t-statistics of six to eight, where the calculation uses probabilities down to roughly 10^-12. If you wish to retain a broad conclusion about p-hacking, provide sensitivity analyses under plausible alternative candidate-statistic distributions, such as t distributions, overdispersed normals, variance mixtures, or an empirically calibrated null. If you instead retain the standard-normal model as a deliberate benchmark, state clearly that the result is conditional on that benchmark and avoid broader claims.

Please improve the provenance and reproducibility of the empirical inputs. Distinguish precisely between originally reported statistics, CZ reconstructed portfolio statistics, and the HLZ distribution simulated from fitted parameters. For the HLZ exercise, report the exact parameter values, simulation size, seed or deterministic replication procedure, Monte Carlo uncertainty, and sensitivity to alternative parameter rows. Explain whether the fitted model targets or preserves the upper-tail bins used here. For CZ, document the sample windows, portfolio and t-statistic construction, exclusions, and sign convention. Because the result is driven by sparse upper-tail bins, report appropriate sampling or simulation uncertainty for the resulting bounds.

It excludes, among other possibilities, adaptive searches that alter the null distribution, misspecified standard errors, heavy-tailed statistics, and mixtures of true and false factors. Nor does the manuscript currently establish that all cited papers endorse the exact conjunction of an all-null universe, independent standard-normal statistics, and t-statistic-only selection. Identify the dataset, target number of publications, candidate-production rate, and publication bound behind every reported figure.

This is a substantial revision, but it does not require abandoning the paper's core idea. A successful revision would replace an unidentified point estimate with a transparent conservative bound, document the empirical calibration, establish the sensitivity or scope of the tail assumption, and align the claims with the model actually analyzed.

### Econometrician Analysis

**Structural Strength**: The paper reduces a vague publication-bias narrative to a transparent selection model and connects the observed histogram of published t-statistics to a concrete quantity—the number of candidate factors required to generate the literature. Equations (1)–(3) make the maintained null and selection mechanism unusually explicit.

**Domain Audit**: The central quantitative estimand is not identified from the published-t-statistic histogram. Equation (3) is invariant to multiplying every publication probability p_i by the same positive constant: the data identify only relative publication probabilities. Nevertheless, the paper declares the model “exactly identified,” implicitly imposes the restriction that the p_i sum to one in deriving Equations (4)–(5), and then treats Equation (6) as an estimated unconditional publication probability. Publication probabilities for mutually exclusive t-statistic bins are not probabilities over the bins and therefore need not sum to one. Under an alternative common scaling consistent with the same observed histogram, Equation (6), publications per year, and years required all change proportionally. The calculation can likely be salvaged as a bound: imposing only 0 <= p_i <= 1 identifies a maximum possible unconditional publication probability, obtained by scaling the largest relative p_i to one, and therefore a lower bound on the required search time. The manuscript should derive that bound rather than call the scale point-identified. It should also narrow its conclusion to selection among correctly calibrated standard-normal null statistics. Equation (1) does not encompass all practices commonly called p-hacking, including adaptive construction that makes the final reported statistic nonstandard, misspecified standard errors, or a fat-tailed empirical null.

**Best-Case Contribution**: The strongest defensible contribution is a sharp thought experiment showing that selective publication alone, when candidate factor t-statistics are correctly calibrated standard-normal null draws, cannot readily reproduce the extreme right tail of published asset-pricing t-statistics without an enormous number of candidate tests. With the selection scale handled as a bound, this is an informative diagnostic against one specific pure-null account of the factor zoo.

Finally, revise the abstract and conclusion to state that the result applies to selection among correctly calibrated normal-null candidate statistics, or provide sensitivity calculations under plausible t-distributions and heavier-tailed nulls if the broader claim about p-hacking is retained.
