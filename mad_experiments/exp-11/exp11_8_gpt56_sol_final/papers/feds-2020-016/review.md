## CONSTRUCTIVE SUMMARY
The paper’s publishable core is a moderately novel institutional dataset and a useful descriptive comparison of governance associations across discretionary and more rule-bound macroprudential instruments. The current execution does not yet support the headline activation interpretation: a persistent policy stock is treated as repeated activation, governance timing is not demonstrated at the country-year level, the analysis rests on few independent events, and the strongest-governance measure partly embeds CCyB-specific authority. In addition, the observational design cannot identify causal governance effects or delegation motives. A dated event-history reconstruction, sparse-event sensitivity analysis, policy-independent governance classification, and consistent associational framing would provide a focused and feasible route to a substantially stronger paper.

## EDITORIAL RATIONALE
Decision: MAJOR REVISION REQUIRED. The strongest credible version of the paper is a comparative, descriptive study linking a hand-collected taxonomy of macroprudential governance to the early cross-country use of Basel III capital buffers. Its most promising contribution is the distinction between the recurring, discretionary CCyB process and the more prescribed SIB-surcharge process. The institutional measures—FSC governance features, committee size, direct authority, and advisory roles—permit a more granular analysis than a simple indicator for whether an FSC exists. Novelty and insight are both MEDIUM: the dataset and policy application are useful and plausibly new in combination, while the conceptual contrast between discretionary and rule-bound instruments is informative but not causally established.

The unanimous RESUBMIT assessments rest on substantive flaws rather than presentational preferences. First, the headline CCyB outcome equals one whenever the buffer is positive. Because almost no country lowered the buffer during the sample, this variable combines first activation, maintenance, subsequent adjustment, and mechanically inherited positive status. The pooled regressions nevertheless interpret it as an activation decision and relate it to contemporaneous financial conditions. This mismatch changes the estimand and may materially affect the governance and credit-growth results. A positive-buffer model can remain as a prevalence analysis, but activation requires a first-event risk-set design.

Second, the manuscript does not establish that FSC existence, cluster attributes, committee size, tools, and authority roles are coded according to their effective dates for every country-year. FSC formation continued during the 2015–2019 outcome window. The record does not prove that later governance was backfilled, but the absence of an observation-level timing rule creates a serious unresolved validity and reproducibility risk. Governance must be measured using arrangements legally in force before each relevant policy decision.

Third, the effective number of activation events is only 14, and the strongest-FSC cluster contains 13 countries. Repeated positive country-years do not create independent activation events. The revised analysis therefore needs transparent event counts by governance category, inference that respects country-level dependence and sparse events, and sensitivity to influential adopters.

Fourth, the strongest-FSC classification partly incorporates whether an FSC can make a formal recommendation on the CCyB—the policy whose use the regression seeks to explain. This is not mechanical identity between regressor and outcome, but it makes the governance measure partly policy-specific. The cluster analysis must be reconstructed or tested after excluding CCyB-specific recommendation authority and using policy-independent governance characteristics.

A separate substantial-rephrasal issue concerns identification. The observational design supplies no exogenous variation in FSC formation, governance strength, committee size, or authority allocation. Crisis controls and a recursive bivariate probit without a credible exclusion restriction do not resolve endogenous institutional selection. Accordingly, the evidence cannot establish that governance affects implementation, that reallocating authority would cause more or better CCyB use, or that elected officials created particular FSCs for functional rather than symbolic delegation. The defensible interpretation is that governance configurations predict or are conditionally associated with policy use, and that functional delegation and coordination costs are mechanisms consistent with—but not selected by—the evidence. The paper also does not assess whether activation improved financial stability or welfare.

There are no unresolved hard blockers, and the critiques are repairable within the existing empirical framework. No separate minor-rephrasal issues or optional extension requests were identified. The defended point is that the current positive-buffer specification need not be discarded: once governance is correctly dated and dependence is handled, it can serve as a secondary prevalence analysis. Likewise, a first-activation model is not an out-of-scope extension but the minimum analysis needed to support the manuscript’s activation claim.

## OFFICIAL LETTER TO THE AUTHOR
Dear Authors,

The paper addresses an important question and has a credible core contribution: it combines a detailed cross-country mapping of financial-stability governance with evidence on the early implementation of Basel III macroprudential capital buffers. The comparison between the recurring, discretionary CCyB process and the more prescribed SIB-surcharge process is particularly promising. The institutional taxonomy also usefully distinguishes FSC existence, governance strength, committee size, direct legal authority, and advisory roles.

Our decision is MAJOR REVISION REQUIRED. All three assessments recommend resubmission. The principal concerns affect the interpretation and potentially the magnitude of the headline results, but they appear repairable using the paper’s existing data framework.

The first issue is the CCyB estimand. The dependent variable equals one in every year in which the buffer is positive, yet the paper repeatedly interprets the estimates as probabilities of activation or as recurring activation decisions. Because releases were rare during the sample, post-activation observations largely record persistence of an earlier policy choice. They should not be treated as additional first activations, and contemporaneous credit growth in those later years need not describe the conditions that produced the original activation. Please estimate a discrete-time first-activation model in which countries remain at risk only while the CCyB is zero, the first positive setting is the event, and post-activation years leave that risk set. Maintenance, subsequent increases, and releases should be treated as distinct outcomes if analyzed. The existing positive-buffer regression may remain as a secondary analysis, but it must be labeled and interpreted as the prevalence of positive CCyB status rather than activation.

Second, please reconstruct and document the governance data as a dated country-year panel. FSC formation continued during the CCyB sample, but the manuscript does not provide an observation-level rule showing that FSC existence, membership, chair, voting arrangements, tools, committee size, direct authority, and advisory roles reflect the arrangements legally effective before each policy observation. Please provide a transparent lineage table reporting the relevant source, effective date, coding rule, and treatment of conflicting information. The present record does not establish that later information was actually backfilled, but it does establish that temporal alignment is insufficiently documented.

Third, the revised inference must reflect the small number of independent activation events. Please report the number of first activations within each governance category, sparse-cell or sparse-event diagnostics, uncertainty that accounts for country-level dependence, and sensitivity to influential adopters, including leave-one-adopter-out checks where feasible. The number of controls and interactions should be disciplined by the limited event count. The 2019 cross-section can remain informative, but it does not substitute for a correctly specified event analysis.

Fourth, the strongest-FSC classification includes formal recommendation authority over the CCyB as one component of good tools. Because CCyB use is the outcome, this makes the headline governance measure partly policy-specific. Please reconstruct the classification without CCyB-specific recommendation authority and report whether the principal results survive. The revised paper should distinguish general governance strength from authority tied directly to the policy being explained.

Finally, the paper requires substantial recalibration of its causal and political-economy claims. The regressions identify conditional associations, not causal effects of governance. Countries’ institutional arrangements may reflect latent intervention preferences, administrative capacity, legal traditions, financial structure, crisis experience, or anticipated risk. The current controls and recursive bivariate model do not provide an exogenous source of governance variation or a credible exclusion restriction. Please replace claims that governance affects, increases, or influences implementation with statements that governance characteristics are associated with or predict observed policy use, conditional on the included covariates. Functional delegation, symbolic delegation, reduced coordination costs, and the creation of new analytical processes may be discussed as interpretations consistent with the patterns, but the analysis does not distinguish among these mechanisms or identify why elected officials created particular FSCs. Nor does a positive CCyB establish that the decision was appropriate or improved financial stability or welfare.

A successful revision would preserve the paper’s strongest contribution while making the estimand, timing, uncertainty, and interpretation internally consistent. We would be willing to evaluate such a revision on its merits.

Sincerely,
Senior Coordinating Editor

# DETAILED ANALYSIS

## ROUND 1: INDEPENDENT AUDITS

### Econometrician Analysis

**Structural Strength**: The paper carefully distinguishes governance concepts that are often conflated: the existence and size of an FSC, clusters based on formal governance features, direct legal authority to set the CCyB, and formal advisory authority. It also compares the recurring and discretionary CCyB decision with the more prescribed SIB surcharge decision. That comparison provides a useful internal check on the proposed institutional mechanism, even though it is not a causal test.

**Domain Audit**: The estimand actually identified is a conditional association between largely time-invariant governance arrangements and whether a country has a positive CCyB during 2015–2019. It is not the causal effect of governance on implementation. Governance is selected rather than assigned: countries with stronger preferences for macroprudential intervention, greater administrative capacity, different political institutions, or different perceived financial risks may both create stronger FSCs and activate the CCyB. Controlling for crisis fiscal costs, macro-financial covariates, and broad country characteristics does not establish conditional exogeneity. The recursive bivariate probit does not solve this problem because the manuscript presents no credible exclusion restriction or other source of exogenous governance variation. In addition, the pooled outcome is highly persistent after activation and is generated by only 14 adopting countries, so the model mixes the timing of first adoption with subsequent years in which the buffer remains positive. The 2019 cross-section is a useful robustness exercise, but the strongest-FSC estimate is only marginally significant there. Thus statements that governance 'affects,' 'increases,' or 'influences' implementation should be replaced by statements about associations or predictive differences. The political-economy results can be described as consistent with functional delegation, but they do not identify why elected officials formed particular FSCs.

**Best-Case Contribution**: The paper provides an early cross-country empirical mapping of how detailed macroprudential governance arrangements correlate with the uptake of Basel III capital buffers. Its strongest credible finding is that positive CCyBs were more prevalent, and more strongly associated with credit growth, in countries whose FSCs had stronger formal governance, fewer member agencies, or direct FSC/ministry authority. The absence of a comparable relationship for SIB surcharges is consistent with the interpretation that governance matters more for recurring, discretionary decisions than for prescribed rulemaking. This is informative descriptive evidence about institutional implementation, not a causal estimate of institutional design.

**Minimum Viable Revision**: Recast the abstract, introduction, results, and conclusion consistently around conditional associations rather than causal effects. Explicitly define the estimand as the difference in predicted CCyB-use probabilities across observed governance structures, conditional on included covariates. State that endogenous institutional selection prevents identifying whether stronger governance causes activation or instead proxies for a country's latent willingness and capacity to intervene. Reframe the functional-versus-symbolic delegation discussion as an interpretation that is consistent with, but not discriminated by, the evidence. To make the descriptive inference more credible, also report country-clustered or country-bootstrap uncertainty and a specification based on first activation or adoption timing, so repeated post-activation observations do not implicitly supply independent policy events.

### Data_Scientist Analysis

**Structural Strength**: The manuscript provides unusually transparent high-level provenance for a hand-constructed cross-country dataset. Appendix C identifies the IMF Macroprudential Policy Survey, BCBS implementation reports, ESRB records, FSB lists, and national authority websites; the authors also explain major sample exclusions, distinguish direct authority from advisory roles, and report country-level CCyB arrangements for adopting countries in Table 2.

**Domain Audit**: The central empirical claim is that stronger FSC governance and fewer member agencies increase countries' use of the CCyB and its responsiveness to credit growth. The longitudinal construction does not cleanly support that interpretation. First, the dependent variable is a policy stock: Decision CCyB equals one in every year in which the buffer remains above zero, not only when authorities activate or raise it. Because almost no country lowered the CCyB during the sample, observations after first activation mechanically remain one and are paired with financial conditions occurring after the original decision. A random intercept does not model this state dependence. Thus, Tables 4A-5 mix activation, maintenance, and inherited policy status while interpreting estimates as probabilities of activation and sensitivity of decisions to credit growth. Second, governance is represented as time-invariant G_i even though the CCyB panel covers 2015-2019 and Figure 1 indicates FSC formation continued through at least 2017. The manuscript does not document that FSC existence, cluster assignment, agency count, or authority roles are dated to each observation. If end-of-period governance was backfilled, post-decision institutional information enters earlier observations. These are repairable but central label and timing problems: the estimates may reflect persistence after adoption or later governance rather than the governance in place when the activation decision was made.

**Best-Case Contribution**: The strongest credible contribution is a new hand-collected comparative dataset linking institutional features of financial stability governance to the observed use of Basel III macroprudential capital buffers. Properly dated and modeled, it could establish an important descriptive fact: countries whose contemporaneous FSCs possess stronger formal decision mechanisms, tools, and fewer coordination points appear more likely to initiate the CCyB, whereas such governance differences are less associated with largely rule-bound SIB surcharge implementation.

**Minimum Viable Revision**: Reconstruct the analysis as a dated country-year panel. Record effective dates for FSC formation, membership, chairs, tools, CCyB authority, and advisory roles; assign governance using only information in force before each policy decision; and publish a country-year lineage table with source URL or document, effective date, coding rule, and reconciliation of conflicting sources. For the activation claim, estimate a discrete-time first-activation model in which countries are at risk while CCyB equals zero, code the first positive decision as the event, and remove post-activation years from that risk set. If maintenance or subsequent increases are also of interest, model those as separate outcomes. Re-run the headline probabilities and credit-growth interactions, with inference appropriate to the small number of activation events. Also provide a complete sample-flow table and sensitivity results for the observations omitted because of missing data or designated as extreme outliers.
