## CONSTRUCTIVE SUMMARY
The paper's promising core is a moderately novel empirical characterization of inflation tails: conventional Phillips-curve variables and credit spreads may contain information that is obscured by mean inflation, with financial conditions appearing especially informative about downside risk. The framework is coherent and the economic insight is useful, but the current evidence does not yet establish the precision of the central asymmetry, the reliability of generated tail probabilities, or the real-time status of the predictive exercise. A focused revision that adds full-pipeline inference, validates the density reconstruction, documents or rebuilds the forecast-origin data, and consistently adopts predictive rather than causal language would preserve the paper's strongest contribution without requiring a new structural or policy model.

## EDITORIAL RATIONALE
Decision: MAJOR REVISION REQUIRED. The strongest credible version of the paper is a reduced-form predictive study of the conditional inflation distribution. It combines an augmented quantile Phillips curve with a fitted skewed-t density to show how a relatively stable conditional mean can coexist with substantial variation in inflation tails, and it presents evidence that credit spreads contain incremental predictive information concentrated in downside inflation risk. The historical applications and U.S.–euro-area comparison make this a potentially useful surveillance framework.

Novelty and insight are both moderate. The empirical application to inflation tails and financial conditions is a meaningful contribution, but the underlying quantile-regression and skewed-t procedures are established methods rather than a new estimator. The principal insight is economically useful: mean inflation dynamics can conceal asymmetric changes in low-inflation probabilities. That insight remains distinct from the manuscript's causal wording and can survive appropriate revision.

The reviewers converge on three substantive flaws. First, the paper does not provide inference for the estimands supporting its headline asymmetry claim. Pointwise intervals for individual quantile coefficients do not test whether the lower-tail credit-spread coefficient differs from its median or upper-tail counterpart. Likewise, the reported Inflation-at-Risk probabilities and baseline-minus-no-credit contrasts are generated through both quantile estimation and skewed-t fitting, but uncertainty is not propagated through that complete pipeline. This is especially important with four-quarter overlapping outcomes and short post-2000 samples. Dependence-preserving inference is therefore required for cross-quantile differences and generated probability contrasts.

Second, the predictive and calibration interpretation is not supported by a sufficiently documented forecast-origin information set. The euro-area unemployment trend is described as an HP-filtered series without a one-sided qualification; U.S. NROU and other series lack vintage and release-timing documentation; and the interpolation, splice, and missing-data rules for expectations are unspecified. The inconsistent definitions of euro-area core HICP must also be reconciled. These issues do not automatically invalidate retrospective conditional associations, but they prevent the expanding-window exercise from being interpreted as feasible real-time surveillance unless it is reconstructed using information available at each origin.

Third, the density-reconstruction step needs validation. Separate quantile regressions may cross, while fitting four skewed-t parameters to the estimated 5th, 25th, 75th, and 95th quantiles imposes shape restrictions on all resulting probabilities. The manuscript does not report crossing diagnostics or sensitivity of policy-relevant tail probabilities to the matching grid, interpolation method, or distributional specification.

A substantial-rephrasal issue also remains. Equation (2) identifies conditional predictive associations, not exogenous financial shocks. Statements that credit conditions generated, pushed down, or historically contributed to inflation-tail movements materially overstate the design. The central claim should consistently be that credit spreads contain incremental predictive information for downside inflation risk, unless a separate identification strategy is supplied. Minor rephrasal should also distinguish retrospective fitted distributions, pseudo-out-of-sample evaluation, and genuine real-time forecasts, and should present Inflation-at-Risk as a surveillance input rather than a policy rule or welfare criterion.

The defended critiques appropriately limit the requested revision. The paper need not add a structural policy model, welfare analysis, instrument rule, or causal experiment to retain a useful predictive contribution. Nor do the vintage concerns invalidate every retrospective full-sample result. These are possible extensions or future work, not conditions for revision. There are no unresolved hard blockers, but the inferential, information-set, and density-validation issues are substantive and preclude acceptance in the present form.

## ROUND 1: INDEPENDENT AUDITS

### Econometrician Analysis

**Structural Strength**: The empirical design is unusually well aligned with the paper's explicitly predictive objective: equation (2) estimates an augmented Phillips curve at multiple conditional quantiles, and equation (3) maps those quantiles into a flexible skewed-t density whose location, scale, skewness, and kurtosis can vary with observed macro-financial conditions. The paper also checks density calibration, alternative inflation measures, alternative financial indicators, and consistency with option-implied inflation probabilities.

**Domain Audit**: The central empirical conclusion—that credit spreads have a particularly strong and persistent association with the lower inflation tail—is not accompanied by inference for the estimands that actually establish that conclusion. The relevant estimands are cross-quantile slope differences, such as delta_0.1 minus delta_0.5, and nonlinear differences in fitted tail probabilities between the baseline and no-credit specifications. Instead, Figure 8 reports only pointwise 68 percent intervals for individual quantile coefficients, while Figures 11-14 and the historical decompositions report generated quantiles, densities, and probability differences without uncertainty bands. Individual coefficient significance does not establish asymmetry across quantiles, and uncertainty from quantile estimation plus skewed-t fitting is not propagated into Inflation-at-Risk. This is especially consequential in the approximately 1999-2017 subsamples, where tail regressions are estimated from roughly 76 quarterly observations with overlapping four-quarter-ahead outcomes and several covariates. The blocks-of-blocks bootstrap is a sensible starting point, but the manuscript needs to apply a horizon-appropriate bootstrap to the complete estimation pipeline and report confidence intervals or simultaneous bands for cross-quantile differences and the resulting Inflation-at-Risk contrasts. Separately, because the regressions identify conditional predictive associations rather than exogenous financial shocks, causal phrases such as credit conditions 'generated' or 'pushed down' inflation risks should be replaced by predictive-association language unless an identification strategy is added.

**Best-Case Contribution**: The strongest defensible contribution is a new empirical characterization of the conditional inflation distribution: standard Phillips-curve variables have heterogeneous associations with different inflation quantiles, and credit spreads contain incremental predictive information concentrated in downside inflation risk, particularly around the global financial crisis and euro-area sovereign-debt crisis. This reframes the missing-disinflation discussion by showing that a stable conditional mean can coexist with substantial movement in lower-tail probabilities.

**Minimum Viable Revision**: Re-estimate uncertainty for the entire pipeline using a bootstrap that preserves the four-quarter overlap and time dependence; report 90 or 95 percent confidence intervals for the cross-quantile coefficient differences, simultaneous bands for the estimated quantile paths, and bands for the baseline-minus-no-credit Inflation-at-Risk probabilities in Figures 11-14. Show that the main results are not an artifact of the chosen 2000 breakpoint. Recast all structural or causal wording and 'historical contribution' language as conditional predictive association or decomposition unless additional identifying variation is supplied. The underlying framework and data can otherwise remain unchanged.

### Data_Scientist Analysis

**Structural Strength**: The manuscript provides an unusually broad data appendix for a macroeconomic tail-risk exercise: it identifies most series, sources, transformations, and alternative measures, and it checks the core result using core CPI, core PCE, cyclically sensitive inflation, several financial-condition measures, and both U.S. and euro-area data. This makes much of the pipeline auditable in principle.

**Domain Audit**: The most consequential data issue is look-ahead and vintage contamination in an analysis repeatedly described as predictive. The euro-area natural rate is explicitly constructed as the full-sample HP-filtered trend of unemployment, so its value at date t uses unemployment observations after t. The U.S. natural-rate series and several national-account price series appear to be downloaded vintages, but the manuscript does not identify vintages or demonstrate that the observations were available at each forecast origin. This conflicts with the expanding-window predictive-density exercise in Section 3.1 and with the interpretation of historical distributions as information available at the time. The problem does not necessarily invalidate the retrospective conditional associations, especially the credit-spread coefficient, but it can improve apparent calibration, alter estimated slack, and change historical tail probabilities around turning points. Data construction is also incomplete in several material respects: the quarterly mapping of semiannual Consensus forecasts is unspecified; the pre-1990 splice to Blanchard, Cerutti, and Summers is not documented; missing-value and release-lag rules are absent; and the euro-area core HICP definition differs between the main text and Appendix A.2. These omissions prevent exact reconstruction and make it unclear whether all predictors were genuinely observable at t.

The cross-country comparison and decomposition of lower-tail inflation risk are useful empirical facts even without a causal interpretation.
