Decision: MAJOR REVISION REQUIRED. The strongest credible version of the paper is an original measurement and reduced-form study showing that the 2018–2019 tariffs intersected extensively with U.S. firms’ import-export networks and that products exported by more exposed firms subsequently experienced weaker export growth. The confidential 2016 firm-trade linkage is a substantial asset: it permits incidence measures across firms, employment, sectors, and exports that public aggregate data cannot provide. The nested exposure measures and combination of lagged confidential moments with current public trade data are promising, although the latter is a useful applied strategy rather than a new estimator. Novelty is HIGH; insight is MEDIUM because the economic narrative is coherent but the empirical proxy and design do not yet isolate the proposed mechanism.

The unanimous RESUBMIT assessments identify substantive, repairable flaws rather than a fatal blocker. First, causal identification and treatment timing are not established. The fixed ITE measure incorporates products eventually tariffed through August 2019 and is interacted with quarters beginning in 2018Q1. This does not align exposure with actual implementation and complicates interpretation of early coefficients. Predetermination in 2016 limits contamination from subsequent sourcing responses, but it does not make tariff assignment random or remove HS6-specific demand, technology, trade, or other contemporaneous shocks correlated with exposure. The formal pre-period is short, and the reported evidence does not establish differential parallel trends. Product fixed effects and broad sector-time controls do not resolve this concern.

Second, the outcome sample is not auditable. Twelve-month log changes require positive exports at both endpoints, yet the manuscript does not specify panel completion, zero and missing-flow treatment, imputation, thresholds, or sample attrition. If zero or disappearing flows are omitted, the estimates condition on continuing positive trade and cannot support the claim that export-market exit is captured. This affects the central estimand and potentially its magnitude.

Third, same-HS4 or same-HS6 import-export overlap measures product-code proximity, not direct input use. The supply-chain interpretation is plausible but not demonstrated, particularly because wholesalers, retailers, re-exporting, differentiated final-goods trade, and unrelated activities within multi-establishment firms offer alternative explanations. Fourth, the preferred count-weighted exposure is not naturally matched to an aggregate export-value outcome in a setting where a small number of firms account for most trade. Predetermined value-, import-cost-, or firm-size-weighted measures and sensitivity to dominant firms are needed. Fifth, inference should reflect the HS4 grouping used in the preferred exposure construction; HS4 or suitable multiway inference should accompany HS6 clustering.

The central overclaims require substantial rephrasal unless stronger analysis resolves these issues. The paper currently moves from an exposure-linked differential to statements that tariffs demonstrably restrained exports. The 60 percent exercise is a fitted decomposition obtained by reallocating nested exposure, not a feasible alternative tariff regime or welfare counterfactual. Likewise, duties divided by employment are imputed accounting costs per worker at affected firms, not estimates of worker, wage, employment, or household incidence. These distinctions are material to the abstract, introduction, results discussion, and conclusion. Minor wording changes alone would not suffice, but there are no unresolved hard blockers and no basis for rejection. Extensions such as a structural welfare model or a fully specified optimal tariff regime are not required; they should instead be reserved for future work unless the authors wish to make those claims.

## OFFICIAL LETTER TO THE AUTHOR
Dear Authors,

The manuscript has a strong and potentially important core contribution. By linking confidential pre-policy firm-trade records to later tariff schedules and public monthly exports, it documents the broad reach of the 2018–2019 tariffs across firms, employment, and export activity. The nested exposure measures also provide an intuitive way to examine whether closer import-export product links are associated with subsequent export performance. The reviewers regard the empirical novelty as high and the descriptive incidence results as independently valuable.

I am inviting a major revision because the current manuscript does not yet support its central causal and policy interpretations. The principal issue is the relationship between treatment timing and identification. Exposure is based on all products eventually tariffed through August 2019 but is interacted with quarterly indicators beginning in 2018Q1. This makes the quarterly coefficients difficult to interpret as tariff dynamics. Using 2016 firm links is helpful because it avoids mechanically incorporating firms’ later responses, but it does not address endogenous product selection into tariffs or product-specific time-varying shocks. The short formal pre-period is also insufficient to establish the relevant counterfactual trends. Please construct exposure that activates with actual tariff implementation dates and, where relevant, predetermined source-country shares and tariff rates. Extend the pre-period as far as feasible and provide a transparent event study, placebo dates, and specifications or comparison groups that address product-specific trends and contemporaneous shocks. Inference should be reported at least at the HS4 level, with suitable multiway alternatives where feasible.

A second essential revision concerns zero and missing trade flows. The manuscript uses twelve-month log changes but does not explain how absent, entering, intermittent, or disappearing country-product-month flows are handled. Please document the completed-panel rule, thresholds, concordance losses, transformations, and observation counts at every filter. Report whether inclusion in the log-growth sample varies with exposure and time. Re-estimate the main results using a method that retains zero flows, or separately estimate extensive and conditional intensive margins. PPML on a completed panel is one possible approach, but it is not uniquely required. The revised interpretation must match the resulting estimand; a continuing-positive-flow regression cannot be said to capture export exit.

Third, the supply-chain mechanism needs validation. Same-HS4 overlap is a plausible proxy for related activity, but it does not establish that the tariffed import is used to produce the exported product. Please test the interpretation using the strongest feasible evidence in your data—for example, manufacturing-only samples, exclusion of wholesalers and retailers or likely re-exporters, input-output mappings, establishment-industry information, or related classifications. If direct validation is not possible, consistently describe the measure as import-export product proximity or potential supply-chain exposure rather than demonstrated input use.

Fourth, reconcile the exposure weighting with the outcome. The preferred measure gives firms equal weight while the dependent variable is total bilateral product export value and trade is highly concentrated. Please compare the count measure with predetermined export-value, import-cost, and firm-size-weighted measures, and assess sensitivity to products dominated by one or a few exporters. Clarify whether the estimand concerns the typical country-product flow, the typical firm, or aggregate export value.

Finally, revise the central claims to match the evidence. Unless the new timing and identification analyses support a causal interpretation, the abstract and main text should present the result as an exposure-linked export-growth differential consistent with supply-chain propagation, not as proof that tariffs caused the full decline. The reported 60 percent result should be labeled a fitted within-sample decomposition, not a feasible tariff-policy or welfare counterfactual. Imputed duties per worker should be described as an accounting scale measure under no-adjustment assumptions, not worker incidence. The paper does not need to estimate welfare or design an alternative tariff schedule, but it should state that it does neither.

These revisions are substantial, but they are feasible within the paper’s existing framework. A successful revision would preserve the valuable descriptive contribution while either materially strengthening the causal analysis or presenting a narrower, accurate reduced-form contribution.

# DETAILED ANALYSIS

## ROUND 1: INDEPENDENT AUDITS

### Econometrician Analysis

**Structural Strength**: The design makes disciplined use of predetermined microdata: tariff exposure is constructed from confidential 2016 firm-trade links, before the election outcome and the 2018-2019 tariff escalation, which substantially reduces mechanical reverse causality from firms changing sourcing after tariffs. The paper also separates U.S. import-tariff exposure from foreign retaliatory tariffs, uses detailed country-product export outcomes, and examines nested exposure definitions that distinguish tightly linked supply chains from any firm-level import exposure.

**Domain Audit**: The paper's actual headline claim is causal: U.S. import tariffs reduced U.S. export growth through firms' supply chains. The current difference-in-differences design does not yet establish the required counterfactual that high- and low-ITE products would have followed parallel export-growth paths absent the tariffs. Tariff assignment was product-specific, while ITE is also product-specific and plausibly correlated with intermediate-input intensity, manufacturing cyclicality, global demand, and other product-level shocks. Product fixed effects in a growth regression remove stable average differences but not differential product trends or product-by-year shocks. The manuscript provides only 2017 as the formal pre-period, and Table 6 shows that ITE already significantly predicts export growth before tariffs. More importantly, the quarterly regressions assign exposure using all products eventually tariffed through August 2019 regardless of when each tariff took effect; the manuscript expressly notes this feature. Thus products tariffed much later are coded as exposed in earlier quarters, making the reported quarterly coefficients difficult to interpret as treatment dynamics. Table 10 also reports negative baseline coefficients as early as 2018Q1 and 2018Q2, although the paper states that the majority of tariffs arrived later. Predetermining exposure addresses behavioral response to tariffs, but it does not make the government's cross-product tariff assignment conditionally random. Consequently, the estimates currently establish a strong differential association around the trade-war period, not a clean causal supply-chain effect. Inference should also be reassessed at the HS4 level because the preferred exposure is explicitly constructed from common HS4 supply-chain categories; clustering only by HS6 may miss within-HS4 residual dependence.

**Best-Case Contribution**: The strongest credible contribution is a novel measurement and reduced-form finding: confidential linked firm-trade data reveal that firms exposed to the 2018-2019 import tariffs accounted for a very large share of U.S. exports and employment, and products exported by firms whose related imports were eventually tariffed experienced unusually weak subsequent export growth, even after controlling for retaliatory tariffs and broad destination-sector shocks. This provides important evidence consistent with supply-chain propagation of protectionism, although the current design does not fully isolate that mechanism causally.

**Minimum Viable Revision**: Use the actual product-country tariff implementation dates and 2016 source-country import shares to construct time-varying exposure rather than coding every eventual tariff as active throughout 2018-2019. Extend the public export panel several years backward and present a full event-study or equivalent pretrend analysis, including placebo treatment dates and economically meaningful product-specific trends or narrower comparison groups. Re-cluster at least by HS4, or report multiway/HS4 inference. Clearly define the estimand as an unweighted country-product growth effect rather than an aggregate export effect. If these exercises do not support parallel trends and timing, revise the abstract, introduction, and conclusion to characterize the estimates as differential associations consistent with supply-chain spillovers rather than causal effects.

### Data_Scientist Analysis

**Structural Strength**: The exposure variables are constructed from confidential, transaction-level firm data observed in 2016, before the tariff increases, which reduces direct contamination by firms' subsequent sourcing responses. The nested narrow, baseline, and broad definitions in equations (1)-(3) also make the measurement assumptions unusually transparent and permit a useful decomposition of exposure by product proximity.

**Domain Audit**: The paper claims that 2018-2019 import tariffs reduced U.S. export growth through firm supply chains, using twelve-month log changes in monthly HS6-country exports as the outcome. The central data problem is that the manuscript never defines how zero, newly appearing, or disappearing trade flows are handled. Equation (6) uses the log of export value in two months, which is defined only when both values are positive. Affected firms may exit export markets, and the paper explicitly says its measure can capture exporters that 'drop out of importing and exporting altogether,' but a country-product-month flow that falls to zero ordinarily cannot enter this log-difference regression without a stated transformation, imputation, or panel-completion rule. Dropping such observations would condition the estimates on continued positive trade and exclude an extensive-margin response that the paper treats as part of the estimated effect. Entry and intermittent monthly shipment patterns can create corresponding selection on the comparison side. Because no sampling, zero-handling, or missing-flow rule is reported, neither the effective estimation population nor the relationship between exposure and regression attrition can be audited. This does not invalidate the exposure construction, but it places the magnitude and interpretation of the headline export-growth estimate in material doubt.

**Best-Case Contribution**: The strongest credible contribution is a new empirical measurement of how extensively the 2018-2019 tariffs intersected with U.S. firms' import-export networks, using scarce confidential Census firm-trade links, together with evidence that continuously observed bilateral product export flows in more exposed sectors subsequently grew more slowly even after controlling for retaliatory tariffs. The descriptive incidence results are independently valuable: affected importers were large, employed substantial shares of manufacturing and retail workers, and accounted for most U.S. exports.

**Minimum Viable Revision**: Document the complete construction of the country-HS6-month estimation panel, including whether absent records mean zero trade, how zeros and one-sided flows are treated, all value or shipment thresholds, concordance losses, and observation counts at each filter. Then re-estimate the main quarterly specification with an estimator that retains the extensive margin, such as PPML in export levels on a completed panel, or a defensible aggregation/transformation that includes zero and disappearing flows. Report attrition and the probability of entering the log-growth sample by ITE level and quarter. If the extensive-margin-inclusive estimates differ, revise the headline magnitude and scope accordingly; if they agree, the principal data concern is resolved.
