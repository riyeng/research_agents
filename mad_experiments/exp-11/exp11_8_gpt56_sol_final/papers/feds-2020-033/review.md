## CONSTRUCTIVE SUMMARY
The paper has a viable and potentially publishable core: a distinctive historical analysis of insurance expansion followed by direct hospital provision in Appalachian coal communities. Its most credible contribution is evidence about sequential interventions—possible insurance-period changes in hospital delivery and infant mortality, followed by substantial hospital-capacity expansion in already insured counties. Novelty and insight are both moderate rather than transformative, but the setting, data integration, and economic organization are useful.

The present version requires major revision because treatment-responsive controls and weights place the headline insurance estimates in doubt, and conventional inference is inadequate reassurance for a hospital intervention involving only ten treated counties. Separately, the paper must abandon the claim that it identifies insurance-hospital complementarity: it identifies hospital entry in highly insured places, not the interaction between hospitals and insurance. Capacity expansion also should not be presented as demonstrated improvement in utilization or health. These are serious but repairable issues, with no unresolved fatal blocker. The shortest credible path is to re-establish the estimates under predetermined controls and fixed or no weights, use few-treated-cluster inference, narrow the causal and policy claims, and correct the infant-mortality calculation.

## EDITORIAL RATIONALE
Decision: MAJOR REVISION REQUIRED. The strongest credible version of the paper is a historically grounded analysis of two sequential UMWA interventions. It may show that counties with greater mining-based exposure to the 1950 insurance program experienced differential changes in hospital delivery and infant mortality before union hospitals opened, and that the later hospital program generated large, persistent net increases in beds and staffing in ten already highly insured coal counties. The sequencing, county-level exposure measure, integration of Vital Statistics and AHA data, and demand-versus-supply framework are promising. The record assesses both novelty and insight as MEDIUM: the setting and assembled evidence are distinctive, but the estimators are standard, insurance eligibility is proxied indirectly, and the policy relevance is context-specific.

The principal substantive flaw is the use of potentially treatment-induced controls and weights. The manuscript states that insurance reduced fertility, yet preferred specifications control for contemporaneous fertility and include a post-insurance interaction with total births. Pregnancy-related regressions are weighted by contemporaneous births, while other outcomes use contemporaneous population controls or weights. These choices may condition on treatment responses, alter the composition of births being compared, and make the represented population depend on treatment. The headline estimates therefore must be re-established using predetermined controls and unweighted or fixed pre-intervention weights. A related identification concern is that baseline mining intensity may capture exposure not only to insurance but also to coal-industry shocks, migration, wages, and other UMWA benefits. The short formal pre-period and disputed 1949 false start limit what pre-trend evidence alone can establish, so the revised paper should address these alternative mining-specific post-1950 changes as far as the existing design permits.

A second substantive flaw concerns inference for the hospital program. Only ten counties received UMWA hospitals, while the manuscript relies on conventional county-clustered standard errors. The hospital results require inference appropriate to few treated clusters, such as a wild-cluster bootstrap or an institutionally defensible randomization or permutation procedure. Leave-one-treated-county-out estimates would also be informative, although they are not a substitute for valid inference.

The central substantial-rephrasal issue is complementarity. The manuscript acknowledges that it cannot separately identify a pure hospital effect from an insurance-hospital interaction and that the hospital coefficient is their sum. It nevertheless labels that coefficient a complementary effect and presents the paper as identifying whether hospitals complement insurance. The defensible interpretation is narrower: the bundled UMWA hospital program expanded capacity in counties already characterized by substantial insurance exposure. The Hill-Burton comparison is suggestive, not a causal test of complementarity, because the programs differed in location, governance, physician recruitment, compensation, and other institutional features. Likewise, increased beds and staffing should not be equated with demonstrated improvements in access, utilization, health, welfare, or insurance effectiveness. The manuscript reports no robust incremental hospital-phase effects on admissions, hospital births, or infant mortality.

A minor but necessary correction concerns the infant-mortality magnitude. The reported coefficient of approximately -21.45 multiplied by the mean mining share of 0.031 implies roughly -0.66 deaths per 1,000 births, not -0.02. This calculation and all associated percentage and gap comparisons should be reconciled throughout, subject to the revised estimates.

### Econometrician Analysis

**Structural Strength**: The empirical architecture usefully exploits the sequencing of the two interventions. The manuscript estimates the 1950 insurance rollout only through 1955 and then studies the 1956 hospital openings using a separate specification, avoiding contamination of the initial insurance estimate by the later hospital construction. It also reports event-study diagnostics, county and year fixed effects, Hill-Burton controls, population-based weights, county-clustered standard errors, and several checks for contemporaneous programs.

**Domain Audit**: The design credibly aims to estimate two local reduced-form effects: differential changes after insurance adoption across counties with different 1950 mining shares, and differential changes after hospital openings in the ten counties receiving UMWA hospitals. It does not, however, identify a causal complementarity or interaction between insurance and hospitals. The hospitals were placed only in locations with substantial insurance exposure, so there is no comparable hospital intervention in low-insurance locations. The manuscript explicitly concedes that a triple-difference decomposition lacks sufficient variation and states that the hospital coefficient is the sum of a pure hospital effect and an insurance-hospital interaction. Nonetheless, it subsequently calls that coefficient the complementary effect and uses it to conclude that hospitals complement insurance. The defensible headline is narrower: adding UMWA hospitals in already highly insured coal-mining counties produced a large net increase in beds and employees, while the earlier insurance rollout was associated with increased hospital births and reduced infant mortality. The separate contribution of insurance-hospital complementarity is unidentified. Similarly, the lower crowd-out estimate relative to Hill-Burton cannot by itself be attributed to insurance complementarity because the programs differed in location selection, hospital management, physician recruitment, and other institutional features. For the hospital estimates, conventional county-clustered inference also deserves caution because treatment occurs in only ten counties; few-treated-cluster randomization or wild-bootstrap inference would materially strengthen the reported significance, although the very large bed and employment estimates may well survive.

**Best-Case Contribution**: The strongest credible contribution is a historically grounded, county-level quasi-experimental study showing that the 1950 UMWA insurance rollout was followed by greater hospital use and lower infant mortality in counties with more mining exposure, and that subsequently adding ten union hospitals in highly insured Appalachian coal communities generated large, persistent net expansions in hospital beds and staffing with limited measured displacement of existing capacity. This provides useful evidence about the sequential rollout of demand- and supply-side health interventions in an underserved setting, even though it does not identify the interaction between them.

**Minimum Viable Revision**: Recast the central claim from an identified insurance-hospital complementarity to the identified sequential reduced-form effects: insurance effects before hospital construction and hospital-entry effects in already highly insured counties. State prominently that the hospital coefficient combines the standalone hospital effect and any interaction with insurance, and avoid attributing the Hill-Burton crowd-out difference specifically to complementarity. Correct the conclusion's infant-mortality arithmetic, which reports 0.02 per 1,000 despite Table 3 implying about 0.66 per 1,000 for the average county. For the ten treated hospital counties, report inference robust to few treated clusters, such as wild-cluster bootstrap or permutation/randomization-style inference.

### Data_Scientist Analysis

**Structural Strength**: The paper triangulates multiple county-year sources—Vital Statistics, American Hospital Association surveys, the Hill-Burton project registry, Census-derived mining employment, and historical hospital locations—and defines separate analysis windows for the 1950 insurance intervention and the 1956 hospital intervention. The event-study presentation and explicit discussion of the 1949 false start, 1954 AHA interpolation, Medicare/Medicaid timing, and Hill-Burton overlap make the empirical pipeline unusually transparent at a conceptual level.

**Domain Audit**: The paper claims causal effects within 399 Appalachian counties from differential exposure to UMWA insurance and from ten UMWA hospital openings. The most consequential construction problem is that the preferred regressions condition and weight on variables the manuscript itself says were changed by treatment. Equation (1) includes contemporaneous fertility, county population, and an interaction between the post-insurance indicator and total births; the justification is that UMWA insurance caused women to have fewer children. Total births and fertility are therefore post-treatment mediators or compositional outcomes, not ordinary confounders. Conditioning on them can change the composition of births and induce selection in estimates of hospital-birth shares and infant mortality. Weighting those regressions by contemporaneous births likewise makes treatment affect observation weights and leaves the target estimand unclear. Related concerns apply to contemporaneous population weights and controls if treatment changed mortality or migration. This does not prove the reported effects are false, but the headline insurance estimates in Table 3 cannot be treated as validated causal estimates until they are reproduced without treatment-induced controls and weights. The hospital analysis also has only ten treated counties, so conventional county-clustered inference should be supplemented by inference suitable for few treated clusters. Finally, the conclusion reports an infant-mortality reduction of 0.02 per 1,000, whereas Table 3's coefficient multiplied by the mean mining share implies approximately 0.66 per 1,000; this numerical inconsistency must be corrected.

**Best-Case Contribution**: The strongest credible version is a historically grounded quasi-experimental study showing that UMWA insurance exposure was associated with increased hospital delivery and lower infant mortality before union hospitals opened, while the subsequent opening of ten hospitals produced a large, persistent net expansion in beds and hospital employment with less apparent crowd-out than the contemporary Hill-Burton program. This provides bounded but valuable evidence that insurance demand and facility supply may complement each other in severely underserved areas.

**Minimum Viable Revision**: Re-estimate every headline specification using only county and year fixed effects plus clearly predetermined controls, omitting contemporaneous fertility, total births, and population as regression controls.
