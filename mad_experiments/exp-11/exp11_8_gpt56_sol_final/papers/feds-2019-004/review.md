### Theorist Analysis

**Structural Strength**: The manuscript consistently separates three distinct economic questions that are often conflated: determination of the money stock, the transmission of monetary policy to aggregate demand and inflation, and the choice of an operating instrument. Its reconstruction of how Brunner's position evolved—from broad-money analysis with Crouch, through advocacy of monetary-base control, to rejection of Sterling M3 as a target—is especially effective and prevents the historical argument from treating monetarism as a fixed doctrine.

**Domain Audit**: The mechanisms are broadly coherent as accounts of Brunner's reasoning, but the manuscript occasionally endorses them more strongly than their equilibrium logic warrants. Most importantly, the claim that debt lengthening would leave aggregate deposit creation unchanged because banks would replace unavailable Treasury bills with private loans is conditional on stable desired reserve-to-deposit ratios, sufficient loan or alternative-asset demand, and no material portfolio-price, interest-rate, or wealth effects from changing debt maturity. The manuscript states that banks would expand assets "until the preexisting desired ratio of reserves to deposits was attained" and calls the resulting criticism of official doctrine convincing, but this is not a general irrelevance result for debt management. Likewise, a monetary-base instrument does not by itself guarantee control of broad money when the multiplier is endogenous and wholesale deposits are important—a limitation the manuscript later recognizes in its discussion of Sterling M3 and Howard (1982). These are best treated as claim-calibration issues because the central historical contribution does not require debt management to be generally irrelevant or monetary-base control to deliver exact broad-money control.

**Best-Case Contribution**: The paper provides a detailed intellectual and policy history showing how Karl Brunner engaged with successive U.K. monetary regimes, how his analysis differed from Radcliffean and cost-push approaches, how his recommendations evolved in response to institutional changes and monetary-aggregate instability, and how the multiple-asset transmission mechanism developed with Allan Meltzer later informed the Bank of England's stated rationale for quantitative easing.
