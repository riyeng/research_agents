## EDITORIAL RATIONALE
Decision: MAJOR REVISION REQUIRED. The strongest credible version of the paper is a policy-relevant scenario analysis that integrates a geocoded portfolio of BRI rail and port projects, multimodal shortest-path routing, and sector-specific values of shipment time. This framework can illuminate network complementarities, geographic heterogeneity, and spillovers beyond project-hosting economies. The contribution is promising, but the present headline aggregates are not yet supported by a stable, interpretable, and auditable estimand.

Substantive flaws: All three reviewers converge on the city-to-country aggregation as the principal defect. Section 3(c) weights each city pair by the sum of its endpoint populations. No probabilistic, freight-flow, trade-exposure, or welfare interpretation is established for this rule. It repeatedly counts each city's population across counterpart cities and makes results sensitive to the number and composition of sampled cities. Moreover, averaging route-level percentage reductions need not equal the percentage change in consistently aggregated pre- and post-BRI shipment-time levels. Because country, regional, BRI-wide, and world estimates are downstream of this procedure, the central magnitudes must be recomputed after the authors define the intended estimand.

Sample construction and numerical traceability also require substantive repair. The abstract reports 1,818 regional cities, while the body reports 1,964. Cities more than 10 km from the network and cities with inconsistent snapping are excluded, but no consolidated attrition table identifies exclusions by country, reason, and scenario. The audits also identify unreconciled regional means in Table 5 versus Section 5, ambiguity about the coverage of Table 6 given the treatment of traded HS categories, and insufficient clarity about the denominators and weighting behind the world averages. These discrepancies prevent independent reconstruction of the headline results.

A further substantive concern is calibration sensitivity. Route selection depends on the four-to-one maritime-preference rule, assumed speeds, the five-percent modal-share tolerance, and port-delay reductions of up to 50 percent. Baseline validation does not establish that estimated changes are robust to these choices. The revised paper must show how central magnitudes and relevant rankings vary under plausible alternatives.

Substantial-rephrasal issues: The manuscript presents its two routing cases as lower and upper bounds, but no bracketing property is demonstrated. Given omitted prices, capacity, reliability, service frequency, interoperability, subsidies, and product characteristics, these are alternative modeled routing scenarios rather than proven bounds on feasible or realized outcomes. Likewise, statements that the BRI “will” reduce shipment times and trade costs convert a conditional simulation—assuming completion and modeled operation of all included projects—into an unconditional forecast. The paper estimates potential gross changes in modeled shipment time and its time-related trade-cost equivalent; it does not establish implementation likelihood, total transport-cost reductions, net welfare, or the desirability of the investment portfolio.

Minor rephrasal issues: Once the central claims are corrected, terminology should remain consistent throughout the title, abstract, tables, figures, results, and conclusion. Every average should identify its observation unit, sample, denominator, and weighting rule, and “trade costs” should be qualified where the calculation concerns the time-related component while tariffs and monetary transport costs are held fixed.

Defended critiques and scope: Reviewers agree that a complete fiscal, debt, procurement, legal, environmental, political-economy, or welfare model is not required to sustain the narrower network-measurement contribution. Those are extensions, provided the manuscript clearly distinguishes gross conditional connectivity effects from implementation feasibility and net social value. The route-level GIS framework has not been shown to be invalid and can survive the revision.

Novelty and insight: Novelty is MEDIUM. The integration of a BRI project inventory, global multimodal routing, and bilateral-sector values of time is a genuine and reusable measurement contribution, although it is not a new identification strategy or routing algorithm. Insight is also MEDIUM. The paper clearly explains network complementarities and the channel from infrastructure to shipment time and time-related trade costs, but interpretation is weakened by the aggregation, sample-accounting, and scenario-labeling problems. No unresolved blocker requires rejection; the defects are serious but repairable within the existing framework.

## OFFICIAL LETTER TO THE AUTHOR
Dear Authors,

The manuscript's strongest contribution is its integrated measurement framework: it maps a portfolio of BRI-associated rail and port projects into a multimodal transport network, computes changes in shortest-path shipment times, and translates those changes into bilateral-sector time-cost equivalents. This is a useful way to study network complementarities, heterogeneous corridor effects, and spillovers to economies that do not host projects. The reviewers regard this contribution as promising and potentially publishable.

I am requesting a major revision because the current aggregation and sample-construction pipeline does not yet support the headline country, regional, and global estimates.

First, please define the estimand underlying each headline average. The current procedure weights a city pair by the sum of the origin and destination populations. As written, this is not a joint endpoint-population measure and is not tied to observed trade or freight exposure. It can also make results depend mechanically on the number and composition of sampled cities. In addition, a weighted average of city-pair percentage reductions generally differs from the percentage change in separately aggregated pre- and post-BRI shipment-time levels. You should therefore select and justify a primary estimand, recompute the central results accordingly, and report clearly labeled alternatives. A population-endpoint interpretation would ordinarily use joint endpoint weights, such as population products; an exposure interpretation would use observed trade or freight weights. For proportional changes, aggregate pre- and post-BRI time levels consistently before calculating the change, or demonstrate the equivalent baseline-time weighting formula. Retain the current sum-weighted index only as a sensitivity comparison if you can explain what it measures.

Second, reconcile the analysis sample and every reported statistic. The abstract reports 1,818 regional cities, whereas the body reports 1,964. Please provide a consolidated attrition table showing candidate cities, exclusions for distance from the network, exclusions for inconsistent snapping, final cities and countries, and whether inclusion differs across scenarios. Reconcile the regional means reported in Table 5 and Section 5, clarify the coverage of Table 6 in light of the traded-HS-category requirement, and attach every headline number to a named analysis table, observation unit, denominator, and weighting rule. Explain explicitly how zero-trade and missing country-sector observations enter the value-of-time merge.

Third, provide sensitivity analysis for the pivotal routing assumptions. At minimum, examine plausible alternatives to the four-to-one maritime-preference threshold, the five-percent modal-share tolerance, transport speeds, border delays, port-delay improvements, and—where feasible—the project-inclusion choices. Report whether the headline magnitudes, geographic patterns, and important rankings are stable. Agreement of baseline modal shares or travel times with external sources is useful validation, but it does not substitute for sensitivity of the estimated changes.

Fourth, improve reproducibility. The revised manuscript and supplement should fully document the project list and versions, project geometries, network sources, snapping and exclusion decisions, routing rules, maritime-preference search procedure and termination conditions, aggregation formulas, and the mapping from routes to country-sector trade costs. Please provide an executable workflow or sufficiently complete code and data documentation to permit independent reconstruction.

I encourage you to organize the response around a complete audit trail from city selection and route construction through country-pair aggregation and global reporting. Because the required changes may materially alter the headline magnitudes, this is a substantive revision rather than a matter of presentation alone.

### Data_Scientist Analysis

**Structural Strength**: The manuscript provides an unusually explicit high-level account of its data pipeline: project-selection criteria, transport-network sources, city thresholds, snapping tolerance, assumed speeds, port and border delays, mode-choice scenarios, city-to-country aggregation, and conversion of time into sectoral trade costs are all described. Separating global and regional databases and lower- and upper-bound scenarios is also a strong design feature.

**Domain Audit**: The paper's actual contribution is a conditional simulation of how a specified set of completed and planned BRI rail and port projects would change modeled shortest-path shipment times and derived trade costs, not an estimate of realized BRI effects. The most consequential data problem is that the sample and aggregation pipeline does not yield a stable, auditable estimand for several headline averages. Cities farther than 10 km from a network feature are excluded, but attrition and post-exclusion country coverage are not reported. Country-pair changes are then constructed using the sum of origin and destination city populations as the city-pair weight. This weighting does not correspond to either the probability of drawing an origin-destination pair from the two populations, which would imply product weights, or observed shipment exposure, which would imply trade or freight weights. It repeatedly counts each city's population across all counterpart cities and can make results depend mechanically on the number and composition of sampled cities. Traceability is further weakened by conflicting regional sample sizes: the abstract reports 1,818 cities, while Sections 2 and 3 report 1,964. Section 5 reports regional city-level means of 1.50 and 2.88 percent, whereas Table 5 reports regional means of 2.86 and 4.45 percent without clearly reconciling the different units and denominators. Because the headline world and BRI averages are downstream of selection and aggregation, their numerical interpretation as representative average shipment-time and trade-cost reductions is presently unsupported, even though the route-level network calculations may remain useful.

**Best-Case Contribution**: The strongest credible version is a new scenario database mapping a documented inventory of BRI-associated rail and port projects into modeled changes in shortest-path travel time for connected sampled cities, then translating those changes into bilateral-sector time-cost equivalents. It usefully demonstrates network spillovers and geographic heterogeneity under transparent infrastructure-completion and transport assumptions.
