Its component-by-component crosswalk is valuable, and the broad finding of a large decline in poverty relative to that benchmark appears to survive the reported exclusion of health insurance. Its insight is also MEDIUM: the framework clarifies why poverty measures diverge, but it does not yet adequately distinguish provision cost, beneficiary welfare, fungible resources, and material need.

The principal substantive flaw is the treatment of health insurance. The preferred 2.3 percent estimate counts the full provision cost of Medicare and Medicaid and employer payments for employer-sponsored insurance as household resources available against a general poverty threshold. Yet these benefits are restricted, provision cost can reflect medical need and medical prices, and the manuscript itself cites evidence that only 20 to 40 percent of Medicaid expenditure may accrue as beneficiary welfare. The issue is quantitatively decisive: excluding health insurance raises the consistently anchored 2017 rate from 2.3 to 4.6 percent. Reporting only zero and full-cost endpoints is insufficient. The manuscript must also explain whether and why person-specific insurance value is pooled across household members. In addition, the historical series relies on probabilistic assignments and backward imputations, including pre-1979 employer-insurance receipt, without reported multiple-draw uncertainty or validation against years in which receipt is observed. ### Data_Scientist Analysis

**Structural Strength**: The data-construction pipeline is unusually transparent for a long historical series. The manuscript provides a component-by-component crosswalk from the Official Poverty Measure to the Full-income Poverty Measure, reports sensitivity to alternative inflation indices, sharing units, taxes, and transfer components, and documents the pre-1979 imputations in Appendix A. Calibrating historical imputations to administrative recipient and expenditure totals is a concrete strength.

**Domain Audit**: The central 2.3 percent estimate is not yet supported as a valid measure of material poverty because the construction counts the full market cost of health insurance as household income available against a general poverty threshold. Medicare and Medicaid values are risk-class average government costs, while employer insurance is valued using employer payments; these amounts are then incorporated into household resources even though they are not fungible for food, housing, or other necessities and may largely reflect medical prices and expected medical need. The manuscript itself cites evidence that only 20 to 40 percent of Medicaid expenditure may accrue as beneficiary welfare, but it neither implements that valuation nor supplies a range between zero, welfare-value, fungible-value, and full-cost treatments. This choice is quantitatively central: Table 1 reports 2.3 percent with health insurance and 4.6 percent without it. It is made more consequential by imputing 1963-1978 insurance receipt from models estimated in 1979, assigning coverage probabilistically, and using those constructed 1963 resources to determine the anchored dollar threshold, without multiple-imputation uncertainty, validation exercises, or sensitivity to historical selection into coverage. The broader finding of substantial absolute-poverty decline likely survives, but the headline level and the claim that the measure captures resources available to lift households above poverty do not follow from the current measurement convention.

**Best-Case Contribution**: The paper constructs a consistent historical accounting exercise asking how many Americans fall below a 1963 absolute standard after incorporating taxes, cash transfers, major in-kind programs, household resource sharing, and a consistent inflation index. Under that explicitly historical and accounting-based standard, poverty declines far more than indicated by the Official Poverty Measure, and the crosswalk identifies which measurement choices drive the difference.

**Minimum Viable Revision**: Recast the headline estimate as a range rather than privileging the full-cost health-insurance valuation. Recompute the entire anchored series under at least zero, 20 percent, 40 percent, and 100 percent valuations of public and employer insurance, with thresholds re-anchored consistently in every specification; explain whether and why an individual's insurance value is shared with other household members. Make the non-health or fungible-value specification the principal material-poverty result unless a stronger welfare basis for full market cost is established. For the 1963-1978 probabilistic imputations, report multiple-draw uncertainty, random-seed and weighting details, aggregate reconciliation, and a validation exercise showing how the backward model performs when applied to years with observed receipt. The conclusion should distinguish descriptive change under a fixed historical standard from the causal contribution of War on Poverty programs.

### Historian Analysis

**Structural Strength**: The manuscript constructs an unusually explicit lineage from the Johnson-era 20 percent baseline through the Official Poverty Measure, the Supplemental Poverty Measure, Wimer et al.'s absolute measure, and Meyer and Sullivan's consumption measure. Table 2 and the crosswalks in Figures 3–8 make the authors' departures from prior measures transparent rather than presenting the Full-income Poverty Measure as an unexplained alternative.

**Domain Audit**: The quantitative contribution is credibly located as a new synthesis of existing poverty-measurement choices, but the historical attribution is too strong. The paper repeatedly claims that the Full-income Poverty Measure evaluates the War on Poverty "as [President Johnson] defined it" and "reflects his terms of engagement." The cited historical evidence establishes that Johnson invoked roughly one fifth of Americans, that his CEA used an absolute benchmark, and that the CEA regarded broader income data as preferable to cash income. It does not establish that Johnson or his advisers defined poverty using the paper's complete modern specification: PCE indexation, household-wide sharing, a square-root equivalence scale, post-tax income, or the full government-cost market value of health insurance. Indeed, the manuscript itself describes baseline poverty thresholds as subjective or arbitrary and recognizes uncertainty over valuing health insurance. The defensible historical claim is therefore that the authors construct one retrospective full-resource, absolute poverty benchmark motivated by selected Johnson-era principles—not that they recover Johnson's uniquely defined standard. This distinction is central because the headline conclusion that the War on Poverty was "largely successful" is presented as following from Johnson's own terms rather than from contestable contemporary measurement choices.

**Best-Case Contribution**: The paper offers a coherent retrospective Full-income Poverty Measure that calibrates the 1963 poverty rate to 19.5 percent, holds the resulting threshold constant using the PCE price index, and incorporates taxes and major cash and in-kind resources. It then uses detailed crosswalks to show why this measure falls to 2.3 percent by 2017 while the Official Poverty Rate remains 12.3 percent. Its strongest contribution is not recovery of a uniquely Johnson-defined poverty concept, but a policy-relevant measurement exercise demonstrating how conclusions about long-run absolute poverty depend on incorporating the modern transfer state, health insurance, sharing units, and alternative inflation indices.

**Minimum Viable Revision**: Recast the title, abstract, introduction, and conclusion so that the FPM is described as an authors' retrospective benchmark motivated by three Johnson-era considerations rather than as President Johnson's own fully specified standard. Separate historically documented commitments—the approximately 20 percent baseline, an absolute orientation, and interest in nonmoney resources—from the authors' modern choices concerning PCE inflation, household sharing, equivalence scales, taxes, and health-insurance valuation. Temper the claim that this is the "first evaluation" and instead state precisely that, among the measures reviewed, it is the first identified by the authors to combine the 1963 Official Poverty Rate anchor with their specified full-income concept. A concise lineage table distinguishing inherited features from author-imposed choices would complete the repair without changing the empirical analysis.
