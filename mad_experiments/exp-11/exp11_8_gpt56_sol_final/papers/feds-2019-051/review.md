## EDITORIAL RATIONALE
Decision: MAJOR REVISION REQUIRED. The strongest credible version of the paper makes an important measurement contribution: nonnegativity corrections applied to noisy daily-data spread estimates can create upward, volatility-dependent distortions when transaction costs are small relative to efficient-price volatility. The manuscript develops this mechanism through limited-dependent-variable intuition, controlled simulations, comparisons with high-frequency benchmarks, and asset-pricing simulations. The reviewers regard the methodological novelty and overall insight as high. In particular, the paper explains why a proxy may correlate with transaction costs yet still be driven substantially by volatility, and it shows that such contamination can produce economically meaningful distortions in calibrated asset-pricing exercises.

The decision turns on substantive execution issues rather than novelty or promise. First, the manuscript has not established its classification of CSM as consistent. Equation (8) averages nonlinear two-day statistics S_t and then censors the average. Under an appropriate law of large numbers with fixed intraday sampling frequency, this converges to max{E(S_t),0}, not automatically to s. Equations (15)-(17) establish the censoring result only for an illustrative variable whose mean is assumed to equal the target; they do not prove E(S_t)=s. This gap materially affects the consistent-versus-inconsistent taxonomy and the recommendation to use CSM over at least one year. It requires a proof, a characterization of the pseudo-true probability limit, a revised moment-based estimator, or a material narrowing of the recommendation.

Second, the empirical benchmark and the low-frequency measures are not shown to target the same transaction-cost aggregate. The TAQ benchmark is a dollar-volume-weighted effective spread, whereas the daily estimators use unweighted closes, returns, highs, and lows. With intraday spread variation and dependence between trade size and market conditions, disagreement can reflect weighting and timing differences as well as estimator bias. The constant-spread simulations establish the proposed mechanism under a common target, but they do not resolve this real-market estimand mismatch. The empirical wedge therefore cannot presently be attributed entirely to bias, and the high-frequency measure should not be called the true spread unless equivalence is demonstrated.

Third, the monthly realized-volatility construction is dimensionally ambiguous. The text appears to sum daily realized volatilities and squared overnight returns, potentially combining volatility and variance units. Because realized volatility is central to the contamination regressions, the exact formula, transformation, units, and affected results must be verified and corrected if necessary.

Minor rephrasal issues follow from these substantive points: distinguish high-frequency benchmark from true spread; distinguish simulation evidence for a possible mechanism from empirical identification of the entire wedge; and avoid calling CSM consistent unless the claim is established. There is no independently supported substantial-rephrasal issue beyond the substantive repairs. The alleged FX full-spread versus half-spread error cannot be adjudicated from the supplied excerpt and should be treated only as a convention check, not as an established defect. Reproducibility requests—Gibbs iterations, burn-in, initialization, retained draws, convergence diagnostics, seeds, software, and simulation implementation—are defended and useful, but are documentation requirements rather than independent evidence that the results are invalid. No unresolved blocker requires rejection, and the central mechanism, equity evidence, and possibility results may survive a focused revision.

## OFFICIAL LETTER TO THE AUTHOR
Dear Authors,

The manuscript offers a strong and potentially important critique of low-frequency transaction-cost measures. Its most compelling contribution is the demonstration that censoring and truncation can cause noisy daily-data spread estimates to inherit an upward, volatility-dependent component when spreads are small relative to price volatility. The progression from statistical intuition to simulations, high-frequency comparisons, and asset-pricing consequences is coherent and insightful. The reviewers also found the challenge to validation based primarily on correlations with high-frequency spreads to be novel and economically relevant.

I am inviting a major revision because three substantive issues prevent the current claims from being considered sound as written.

1. Establish or withdraw the consistency claim for CSM. Equation (8) averages nonlinear two-day estimates S_t and then censors the average. With fixed intraday sampling frequency, averaging delivers E(S_t) in the limit under suitable regularity conditions; it does not establish that this limit equals s. The normal illustration in equations (15)-(17) assumes that the averaged variable is already centered on its target and therefore cannot prove CSM consistency. Please either derive the relevant probability limit under the stated data-generating process, characterize any asymptotic pseudo-bias over the empirically relevant spread-volatility region, or replace CSM with a construction that averages identifying range moments before applying the nonlinear inversion. If none of these routes supports consistency, remove that label and materially narrow the recommendation that annual CSM is a remedy. Please state the asymptotic sequence and dependence conditions explicitly.

2. Align or qualify the empirical estimands. The TAQ benchmark is dollar-volume weighted, while the low-frequency measures use unweighted closes, returns, highs, and lows. These objects need not coincide when spreads vary intraday or trade size depends on market conditions. Using the transaction-level data already available, report alternative high-frequency benchmarks that more closely match the daily estimators—at minimum equal-trade-weighted effective spreads and a closing-window benchmark, with any feasible time-weighted alternative also informative. Show how the principal wedges and volatility coefficients change. For high-low estimators, discuss the remaining mismatch that cannot be eliminated by simple trade weighting. Unless target equivalence is demonstrated, describe the empirical comparator as a high-frequency benchmark rather than the true spread, and do not interpret the entire difference as estimator bias.

3. Verify realized-volatility construction and units. The description of monthly realized volatility appears to combine daily realized volatilities with squared overnight returns. Please state whether the daily object is variance or its square root, provide the exact daily-to-monthly formula, reconcile the units and reported magnitudes, and rerun affected regressions if the implementation is not dimensionally consistent.

The revision should also improve reproducibility. Report the Gibbs-sampler iteration count, burn-in, initialization, retained draws, and convergence checks; provide random seeds, software information, and sufficiently detailed code or pseudocode; and explain how the large simulations were executed, including any streaming, aggregation, or parallelization. These are documentation requirements and should be kept conceptually separate from the substantive validity issues above.

Finally, please verify that all equity and foreign-exchange quantities use a consistent full-spread or half-spread convention. The supplied material does not permit us to conclude that an FX factor-of-two error occurred, so this is a request for confirmation rather than a finding that the implementation is wrong.

A successful revision need not abandon the paper's central message. The simulations already provide credible evidence that nonnegativity corrections can generate volatility contamination under a common constant-spread target, and the asset-pricing exercises remain useful demonstrations of possible consequences. The revision must, however, separate that established mechanism from the unsupported CSM consistency claim and from empirical differences that may partly reflect nonaligned transaction-cost aggregates.

Sincerely,
Senior Coordinating Editor

## ROUND 1: INDEPENDENT AUDITS

### Econometrician Analysis

**Structural Strength**: The paper triangulates its central measurement argument unusually well: it derives the censoring intuition, studies controlled Monte Carlo designs, benchmarks daily-data proxies against TAQ and EBS transaction-cost measures, separates time-series from cross-sectional variation, and then demonstrates downstream asset-pricing consequences.

**Domain Audit**: The main descriptive claim is well supported: in the stated U.S. equity and major-FX samples, low-frequency spread proxies are much larger than high-frequency benchmarks and load strongly on realized volatility. No causal identification is required for that claim, and the paper generally uses appropriately noncausal language. The decision-relevant econometric defect concerns the repeated classification of CSM as a consistent estimator. CSM is defined in equation (8) as max{average(St),0}, where each St is a nonlinear two-day spread estimate from equations (4)-(7). As T grows with the number of intraday observations n fixed, the law of large numbers delivers average(St) converging to E(St), not necessarily to s. Unlike ARM, for which the manuscript states E(delta_t)=s^2, and the Roll estimator, whose underlying covariance identifies s^2, the manuscript supplies neither an unbiased moment E(St)=s nor a consistency proof for CSM. Equations (15)-(17) concern an illustrative iid-normal variable with mean mu and therefore cannot establish that E(St)=s in the actual CS construction. This matters because the paper calls CSM consistent and makes its principal practical recommendation in favor of the 'consistent version' of Corwin-Schultz with at least one year of data. Table 1 itself does not settle the issue and sometimes leaves nontrivial discrepancies at T=251. The central diagnosis of volatility contamination likely survives, but the asymptotic taxonomy and preferred remedy are not currently established.

**Best-Case Contribution**: The strongest credible contribution is a reframing of validation evidence for daily-data transaction-cost proxies: their correlation with high-frequency spreads can be misleading because nonnegativity corrections make them load on return volatility, especially when spreads are small relative to volatility. The paper documents that mechanism in simulations and in large equity and proprietary FX samples, and shows through calibrated asset-pricing experiments that the resulting measurement error can materially distort liquidity-risk inference.

**Minimum Viable Revision**: Prove the probability limit of CSM under the stated Roll/CS data-generating process, explicitly addressing fixed n as T tends to infinity. If E(St) is not exactly s, stop calling CSM consistent, quantify its asymptotic pseudo-bias over the empirically relevant spread-volatility region, and narrow the recommendation accordingly. Alternatively, construct and evaluate a genuinely moment-based long-window CS estimator that averages the identifying range moments before applying the nonlinear transformation. The descriptive empirical findings can otherwise remain, with 'true spread' consistently relabeled as the high-frequency benchmark where appropriate.

### Data_Scientist Analysis

**Structural Strength**: The paper triangulates the measurement question unusually well: it derives the censoring/truncation mechanism, verifies it in controlled Monte Carlo experiments, compares daily-data measures with transaction-level benchmarks in two asset classes, separates monthly from annual construction windows, and carries the resulting measurement error into asset-pricing simulations. The equity pipeline also documents constituent selection, CRSP-TAQ matching, quote and trade filters, treatment of splits and distributions, realized-volatility construction, winsorization, and sample sizes.

**Domain Audit**: The paper's actual claim is that popular daily-data spread estimators are strongly upward biased and become proxies for volatility when true transaction costs are small relative to price volatility, as in 2003-2017 S&P 1500 equities and 2008-2015 major FX pairs. The most important data-measurement problem is that the empirical benchmark and the low-frequency estimators do not clearly target the same estimand. The TAQ and EBS benchmarks are dollar-volume-weighted effective spreads, whereas Roll, Hasbrouck, Corwin-Schultz, and Abdi-Ranaldo use closing prices, extrema, or unweighted daily moments and contain no volume weights. In markets with intraday spread variation and dependence between trade size, volatility, and spreads, a daily high-low or closing-price statistic need not estimate the dollar-volume-weighted average transaction cost. Consequently, the large empirical wedge cannot, as currently constructed, be interpreted entirely as estimator bias. This mismatch also matters for the regressions: measurement error or estimand differences in the high-frequency spread can attenuate its coefficient and allow realized volatility, which is correlated with both spreads and intraday trading conditions, to absorb explanatory power. The simulations convincingly establish the proposed censoring mechanism under a constant-spread Roll model, so this issue does not invalidate the theoretical result, but it weakens the empirical claim about the magnitude and source of bias in actual markets until common-target benchmarks are reported.

**Best-Case Contribution**: The strongest credible contribution is a general measurement warning with substantial practical importance: imposing non-negativity on noisy daily-data estimates creates a volatility-dependent positive bias when spreads are small relative to efficient-price volatility, and this contamination can materially distort liquidity and volatility-risk asset-pricing exercises. The paper further shows that longer samples help consistent constructions, especially CSM, but cannot repair measures that censor or truncate individual estimates before averaging.

**Minimum Viable Revision**: Explicitly define the empirical target transaction-cost estimand and rerun the core descriptive and regression tables using high-frequency benchmarks with alternative weightings that are closer to what the daily estimators can represent, at minimum equal-weighted per-trade effective spreads alongside the current dollar-volume-weighted measure and a closing-window benchmark for close-based estimators. Report how much of each low-frequency/high-frequency wedge and volatility coefficient survives across these targets. Discuss the irreducible mismatch for high-low estimators, distinguish model-parameter bias from disagreement with a particular trading-cost aggregate, and temper claims about the entire empirical wedge if results depend materially on benchmark weighting. The existing TAQ and EBS data appear sufficient for this repair.
