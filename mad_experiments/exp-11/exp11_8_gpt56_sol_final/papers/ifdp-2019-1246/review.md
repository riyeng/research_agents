## EDITORIAL RATIONALE
Decision: MAJOR REVISION REQUIRED. The strongest credible version of the paper is a novel, data-intensive empirical study showing that dollar appreciation is conditionally associated with tighter U.S. C&I credit conditions, weaker secondary-loan prices and liquidity, and outflows from specialized loan funds, with stronger bank responses where reliance on loan sales is greater. The combination of FR Y-14, confidential SLOOS, LSTA, EPFR, and bank balance-sheet data is the principal contribution. The held-for-sale heterogeneity, SLOOS reasons, and secondary-market evidence are especially promising because they form a coherent institutional account rather than a single aggregate correlation. Novelty is best assessed as MEDIUM, with some reviewers seeing a stronger empirical contribution; insight is MEDIUM because the proposed transmission sequence is clear, but the reason institutional investors respond to the dollar remains unresolved and narrative coherence is sometimes mistaken for identification.

The substantive flaws are material but repairable. First, the manuscript interprets an exactly identified IV calculation as showing that secondary-market prices fully explain the reduced form. With the dollar as the sole excluded instrument for loan prices, the IV coefficient multiplied by the first-stage coefficient mechanically reconstructs the reduced-form coefficient. This is not a mediation test, does not validate exclusion, and cannot establish that the secondary-market channel is unique or complete. Second, the foreign-monetary-announcement IV is not presently adequate for causal claims: the record reports 64 observations, no excluded-instrument first-stage F statistic, no weak-IV-robust confidence interval, and only the euro-area surprise as individually significant in the first stage. The manuscript itself acknowledges possible direct foreign-policy effects on U.S. credit conditions; direct global-risk, funding, activity, demand, and interest-rate spillovers remain unresolved. Third, the headline Y-14 quantity requires a reporting-lineage audit. New loans are identified through origination dates and first appearance in quarterly reports, while the mechanism emphasizes sales within 30 days. The record does not establish that rapidly and fully distributed loans are omitted, but neither does the manuscript establish their observability. Because reporting may depend on retention or distribution, the 10 percent estimate cannot yet be presented as a comprehensive effect on total originations. The use of 16 continuously reporting banks also requires representativeness and scaling validation. Finally, the safer-borrower result needs disclosure and sensitivity analysis for missing-PD imputation and the exclusion of PDs above 10 percent.

The central substantial-rephrasal issue is causal calibration. Statements that dollar appreciation ‘reduces’ originations, that the decline ‘is driven by’ secondary-market demand, that institutional investors’ growth ‘has led to’ the channel, and that the channel ‘fully explains’ lending materially exceed the designs. The $100 billion counterfactual likewise lacks a secure causal and population-scaling basis. The defensible framing is that the variables are associated in a pattern consistent with a secondary-market transmission mechanism. Policy claims must also be narrowed: the evidence supports monitoring and improved data collection, but not a welfare case for regulation or a claim that intervention would ensure stable credit supply. Minor rephrasal issues include clearly identifying the 16-bank, 25-quarter scope of the headline estimate; stating that the SLOOS relationship becomes significant after recession/GFC quarters are excluded; and ensuring that referenced specifications and tables are fully reported with samples, standard errors, and diagnostics.

Defended critiques have been incorporated proportionately. The Y-14 timing mismatch is a serious validation concern, not affirmative proof that sold loans are absent. Likewise, invalidating the Table 12 interpretation does not erase the independent SLOOS, LSTA, EPFR, exposure-heterogeneity, or reported borrower-time evidence. A welfare model or detailed regulatory design is not required if the policy conclusion is confined to surveillance and data needs. No unresolved hard blocker or extension request is present. The decision therefore reflects reviewer consensus for resubmission and the existence of genuine but remediable identification and measurement problems, rather than a judgment that the empirical contribution is unsound in its entirety.

## OFFICIAL LETTER TO THE AUTHOR
Dear Authors,

The manuscript assembles an unusually rich set of supervisory, survey, secondary-market, fund-flow, and bank balance-sheet data to study the relationship among the dollar, institutional demand for corporate loans, and U.S. bank credit conditions. Its strongest contribution is the coherent empirical pattern across these sources: dollar appreciation is associated with weaker secondary-loan prices and liquidity, outflows from specialized loan funds, and tighter C&I credit conditions, with stronger responses among banks more reliant on loan sales. The SLOOS reasons and held-for-sale heterogeneity are particularly useful in connecting aggregate movements to the proposed institutional mechanism.

Our decision is MAJOR REVISION REQUIRED. The paper is promising, but its headline causal and complete-mediation claims are not supported by the reported designs, and the central Y-14 quantity requires additional validation.

The most important econometric issue concerns the claim that the secondary-market channel ‘fully explains’ the reduced form. When the dollar is the sole excluded instrument for the loan-price index, the product of the first-stage and exactly identified IV coefficients mechanically reproduces the reduced-form coefficient. The numerical equality therefore cannot serve as evidence of complete mediation, cannot rule out direct dollar effects or common global shocks, and does not validate the exclusion restriction. Please remove this interpretation and present the exercise, if retained, as an algebraic reparameterization rather than a mediation test. A claim of mediation would require a genuinely discriminating design that permits and identifies other channels.

The foreign-monetary-announcement IV also needs substantial revision. The manuscript acknowledges possible direct effects of foreign monetary policy on U.S. credit conditions. The current record does not rule out spillovers through global risk appetite, funding conditions, expected activity, borrower demand, or interest rates. Moreover, the reported implementation has only 64 observations, lacks an excluded-instrument first-stage F statistic and weak-IV-robust confidence intervals, and appears to have only the euro-area surprise individually significant in the first stage. If you retain this design, report complete first-stage diagnostics, partial explanatory power, weak-IV-robust inference, and results for instruments separately, and provide a much sharper exclusion argument. Unless these concerns are convincingly resolved, the IV should be described only as additional evidence consistent with the mechanism, not as establishing causality even for credit standards.

A second major issue is the construction and coverage of the FR Y-14 origination measure. The paper defines a new loan using its origination date and first appearance in quarterly reports, while emphasizing that loans may be sold within 30 days. Please document whether commitments originated and fully distributed before the reporting date remain observable, distinguish commitments from retained exposures, explain the treatment of syndications, refinancing, and amendments, and test sensitivity to reporting timing. Reconcile the constructed totals to an external transaction-level benchmark if feasible, and quantify the consequences of restricting the sample to 16 continuously reporting banks. The present record does not prove that rapidly sold loans are omitted, but the manuscript must establish coverage before interpreting the estimate as total originations or scaling it to a market-wide dollar amount. Otherwise, describe the outcome strictly as observed Y-14 originations and remove unsupported population counterfactuals.

Please also report the shares of observations with missing PDs and PDs above 10 percent, whether those shares vary with the dollar, and robustness to raw ratings, alternative imputations, medians or quantiles, and alternative trimming or winsorization. These checks are needed before the paper can conclude that banks shift toward safer borrowers.

The abstract, introduction, results discussion, and conclusion require substantial recalibration. Replace claims that dollar appreciation ‘reduces’ lending, that the decline ‘is driven by’ secondary-market demand, that institutional investors’ growth ‘has led to’ the channel, and that the channel ‘fully explains’ the relationship with language describing conditional associations and evidence consistent with the proposed mechanism. Clearly disclose that the headline origination estimate uses 16 banks over 25 quarters and that the SLOOS dollar coefficient is insignificant in the full sample and becomes significant after recession/GFC observations are excluded. The $100 billion counterfactual should be removed or explicitly labeled as a noncausal calculation limited to validated sample coverage.

Finally, narrow the policy conclusion to the supported implication: nonbank loan-market exposures merit monitoring and improved data collection. The paper does not provide a welfare benchmark, market-failure test, policy counterfactual, regulatory instrument, legal-authority analysis, or assessment of costs and market migration. Those elements would be needed for a stronger intervention claim, but they are not required if the conclusion is confined to surveillance and information needs.

A successful revision need not abandon the paper’s central empirical contribution. The independent evidence from SLOOS responses, secondary-market prices and liquidity, fund flows, and bank exposure heterogeneity remains informative after the causal and complete-mediation claims are removed. The revision should make that bounded but meaningful contribution the center of the paper and provide the data and econometric validation necessary for readers to assess its quantitative scope.

Sincerely,
Senior Coordinating Editor

### Econometrician Analysis

**Structural Strength**: The paper triangulates the proposed secondary-market mechanism across unusually rich and complementary sources: confidential FR Y-14 loan-level data, confidential bank-level SLOOS responses, LSTA loan prices and bid-ask spreads, EPFR mutual-fund flows, bank balance sheets, and Shared National Credit buyer composition. Particularly useful are the exposure-based tests in Table 7 and the borrower-time fixed-effect specification in Table 16, which distinguish the proposed channel from aggregate borrower demand more effectively than the aggregate time-series regressions alone.

**Domain Audit**: The strongest defensible estimand is an association: outside recession quarters, dollar appreciation predicts tighter C&I credit conditions, weaker secondary-loan prices and liquidity, and outflows from bank-loan mutual funds, with stronger responses among banks that hold more loans for sale. The manuscript instead repeatedly gives this relationship a causal and mechanistic interpretation. The abstract says that a 2.5-point dollar increase "reduces" originations by 10 percent and that the decline "is driven by" secondary-market demand; the introduction says institutional investors' growing role "has led to the emergence" of the channel. Most identifying variation is aggregate time-series variation in an endogenous exchange rate. The principal originations estimate has only 25 quarterly observations and 16 continuously reporting stress-test banks, and the SLOOS dollar coefficient is insignificant in the full sample in Table 3, becoming significant only after recession/GFC quarters are excluded. The foreign-monetary-announcement IV does not resolve the central problem: it is reported only for SLOOS standards, provides no excluded-instrument first-stage F statistic or weak-IV-robust inference, has 64 observations and several instruments with only the euro-area coefficient individually significant, and relies on an exclusion restriction under which foreign monetary surprises affect U.S. credit conditions only through the dollar. The manuscript acknowledges possible direct interest-rate spillovers but does not establish that all direct financial, risk, or global-demand spillovers have the asserted opposite sign. Moreover, Table 12 cannot demonstrate that the secondary-market channel "fully explains" the reduced form: in an exactly identified IV using the dollar as the instrument for loan prices, the product of the first-stage and IV coefficients mechanically reproduces the dollar reduced form. The exposure heterogeneity, price, liquidity, and fund-flow evidence is highly suggestive of the channel, but it does not separately identify mediation or rule out a common global risk shock that simultaneously moves the dollar, institutional portfolios, loan prices, and bank lending. The results should therefore be presented as a coherent set of conditional associations consistent with the secondary-market channel, not as causal estimates of dollar appreciation or proof that this channel fully mediates the relationship.

**Best-Case Contribution**: The paper documents a novel and economically meaningful macro-financial pattern using scarce supervisory data: dollar appreciation is associated with fewer and safer C&I loan originations by major U.S. banks, tighter reported standards outside recessions, lower secondary-loan prices and liquidity, and outflows from U.S. bank-loan mutual funds. Cross-bank exposure heterogeneity and borrower-time fixed effects make the evidence particularly consistent with institutional-investor demand in the secondary loan market transmitting global financial conditions to bank lending.

**Minimum Viable Revision**: Recast the abstract, introduction, conclusion, and quantitative discussion around prediction and association rather than causal effects: replace "reduces," "driven by," "fully explain," and "has led to" with language such as "is associated with" and "is consistent with." State explicitly that the main originations estimand concerns 16 major stress-test banks over 25 quarters and that the SLOOS result is conditional on excluding recessions. Remove the mediation interpretation of Table 12 or explain that its equality with the reduced form is an IV identity rather than evidence of full channel accounting. If causal language is retained, report first-stage and weak-IV diagnostics, use weak-IV-robust confidence intervals, sharply defend the foreign-monetary-shock exclusion restriction, and distinguish dollar effects from direct global-risk and foreign-policy spillovers.
