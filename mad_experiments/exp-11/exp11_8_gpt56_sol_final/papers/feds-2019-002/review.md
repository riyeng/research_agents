## EDITORIAL RATIONALE
Decision: MAJOR REVISION REQUIRED. The strongest credible version of the paper is a useful diagnostic study of the internal statistical coherence of conditional judgmental forecasts. Its principal contribution is the distinction between “absorption”—whether revisions to interest-rate projections covary with revisions to other forecast components—and “fidelity”—whether those revisions predict subsequent forecast errors. Applying this framework across Greenbook, Blue Chip, and SPF forecasts produces informative descriptive evidence: macroeconomic and interest-rate projections often revise together, while detected pairwise forecast-error predictability is less pervasive but includes notable recurring exceptions. The framework has MEDIUM methodological novelty because it organizes familiar forecast-efficiency moments in a clear and portable way rather than introducing a new estimator or identification strategy. The cross-survey application is useful but empirically bounded, relying on established datasets and standard regressions. The paper’s insight is MEDIUM: the absorption/fidelity distinction clarifies the question, but the current implementation and interpretation do not yet establish genuine or efficient conditioning.

The decisive substantive flaw is an estimand mismatch. Equation (2) formulates fidelity relative to forecast-date information Z_t, but Tables 7–9 condition on NBER status at t+h and the realized error in the conditioning interest-rate forecast. Both are learned after the forecast is made. Tables 4–6 similarly use future NBER status in revision regressions. These controls can change the partial association between interest-rate revisions and macroeconomic forecast errors, so the reported coefficients are not direct tests of the stated ex-ante fidelity restriction. The existing specifications may be informative as explicitly labeled ex-post error decompositions, but they cannot support the headline ex-ante interpretation without additional assumptions. This issue was independently identified at high confidence and conceded in the debate.

A second substantive flaw concerns inference. The manuscript evaluates many survey-rate-variable-horizon coefficients separately and draws a broad conclusion largely from non-rejection. Yet Tables 7–9 contain recurring significant patterns, including longer-horizon Greenbook unemployment errors, SPF long-rate errors following three-month-rate revisions, and extensive cross-rate predictability. The paper provides no omnibus tests, multiplicity adjustment, equivalence bounds, power analysis, or economically calibrated confidence intervals. Individual non-rejections therefore do not establish a joint efficiency claim.

A third substantive issue is dependence and reproducibility. Monthly Blue Chip, typically twice-quarterly Greenbook, and quarterly SPF forecasts generate repeated forecasts for common target quarters and overlapping horizons. The manuscript states that Newey-West standard errors are used but does not report lag lengths, the selection rule, finite-sample treatment, target-quarter clustering, or a suitable block-bootstrap alternative. It also does not provide a sufficiently precise observation-level algorithm for matching survey dates, information cutoffs, target quarters, horizons, and cross-survey revisions. These omissions prevent readers from verifying that the dependence correction and cross-frequency joins support the reported inference.

There is also a conceptual limitation that must be defended rather than obscured. Absorption and fidelity are useful statistical diagnostics, but neither identifies whether an interest-rate path was actually used to construct the macroeconomic forecast. Co-revision may reflect common news, and forecast-error orthogonality may hold even if the rate path was ignored. The paper already disclaims a causal interpretation of absorption; it should consistently present the framework as testing specified necessary moments of linear coherence, not as a sufficient test of genuine conditionality.

The central wording issue is consequential but secondary to re-estimation. Statements that rates were “efficiently” or “properly” incorporated exceed what pairwise linear moments can show and should be replaced by claims about failure to detect specified systematic linear predictability, conditional on the revised joint evidence. The analysis does not validate the assumed policy path, identify monetary transmission, measure decision losses, or establish institutional performance. Likewise, because the paper studies consensus forecasts, claims about what individual forecasters believe or do should be narrowed unless respondent composition is documented. A fixed-panel individual-forecaster analysis would be an extension rather than a mandatory redesign; the aggregate consensus remains a legitimate estimand.

No unresolved hard blocker is present, and the defects appear repairable within the existing data and framework. The appropriate developmental decision is therefore major revision rather than rejection.

The paper begins from a valuable question: when a judgmental forecast includes an interest-rate path, do revisions to that path appear coherently in the rest of the forecast, and do they reveal subsequent forecast errors? The distinction between absorption and fidelity gives this question a clear empirical structure, and the comparison of Greenbook, Blue Chip, and SPF forecasts yields potentially useful descriptive evidence. In particular, the strong revision comovement and the heterogeneous forecast-error patterns across surveys, rates, variables, and horizons merit careful presentation.

That equation conditions on Z_t, but Tables 7–9 include NBER status at t+h and the realized forecast error of the conditioning interest rate. Both are post-forecast outcomes. Tables 4–6 likewise use future recession status in regressions of revisions made at t. Because these controls can materially alter the coefficient on the interest-rate revision, the resulting estimates are ex-post partial associations rather than direct tests of forecast-date fidelity.

Please make specifications using only information observable at the forecast date the primary tests of equations (1) and (2). The current future-controlled regressions may be retained as a separately labeled ex-post error-decomposition exercise, provided you explain precisely what those coefficients identify and do not present them as the primary ex-ante test.

The second major concern is the move from many individual coefficient estimates to a broad conclusion about efficient incorporation. Tables 7–9 contain recurring significant exceptions, including longer-horizon Greenbook unemployment results, SPF long-rate errors following short-rate revisions, and cross-rate predictability. Please report joint tests organized at meaningful levels—for example, by survey and conditioning rate, with outcome- or horizon-level blocks and an overall system test where feasible. Non-rejection alone should not be treated as affirmative evidence of efficiency.

Third, the dependence structure and data construction require substantially more documentation. State the Newey-West lag lengths and selection rule for every relevant design, and explain how inference accommodates repeated forecasts for the same target quarter and overlapping horizons. These are serious issues, but they do not require abandoning the project. A revision that aligns the empirical specifications with the forecast-date information set, supplies dependence-robust joint inference, documents the data pipeline, and narrows the interpretation could preserve a meaningful contribution. ### Econometrician Analysis

**Structural Strength**: The paper clearly separates two empirically distinct implications of conditional forecasting: comovement of revisions ('absorption') and the absence of linear predictability of subsequent forecast errors from conditioning-variable revisions ('fidelity'). It also explicitly avoids interpreting the revision regressions causally and compares the same diagnostics across three influential forecast sources.

**Domain Audit**: The regressions identify pairwise linear covariance restrictions, but the manuscript draws its central efficiency conclusion largely from failures to reject individual coefficients across many horizons, outcomes, rates, and surveys. No joint test of the fidelity restrictions, multiple-testing adjustment, power or equivalence analysis, or complete dependence specification is reported. This matters because Tables 7-9 contain recurring rejections rather than uniformly null results, including Greenbook unemployment errors at longer horizons, SPF long-rate errors following three-month-rate revisions, and substantial cross-rate predictability. With monthly or twice-quarterly forecasts for overlapping quarterly targets, conventional Newey-West inference is valid only under an appropriate lag and dependence design, neither of which is documented. Moreover, consensus revisions can reflect changes in respondent composition rather than revisions by a fixed forecaster; the manuscript does not explain how this is addressed for Blue Chip or SPF. Consequently, the evidence supports the narrower descriptive claim that many individual linear predictability coefficients are imprecisely estimated, but it does not yet establish the joint statement that interest-rate projections are efficiently or faithfully incorporated.

**Best-Case Contribution**: The paper provides a useful, portable diagnostic framework for asking whether revisions to an assumed financial path move with the rest of a judgmental forecast and whether those revisions predict subsequent forecast errors. Applied to Greenbook, Blue Chip, and SPF forecasts from 1984-2011, it documents substantial revision comovement and relatively limited pairwise evidence of macroeconomic forecast-error predictability.

**Minimum Viable Revision**: Estimate and report omnibus tests of the fidelity restrictions by survey, conditioning rate, outcome block, and overall system, using inference that explicitly accommodates repeated forecasts for the same target quarter and overlapping horizons. The conclusion should then follow the joint tests and distinguish failure to detect linear predictability from affirmative proof of correct conditioning.
